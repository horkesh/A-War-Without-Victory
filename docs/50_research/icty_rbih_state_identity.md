# RBiH State Identity: "What Is Bosnia?"

## ICTY-Sourced Research for AWWV Player Decision

**Purpose:** Inform the player decision where the RBiH leader chooses between civic multi-ethnic state, Bosniak national state, or pragmatic hybrid.

---

## 1. The May 1992 Presidency Platform

### Independence and Recognition Timeline

- **29 Feb - 1 Mar 1992:** Referendum on independence held. Largely boycotted by Bosnian Serbs; overwhelming majority voted for independence. (Karadzic Judgment, Para. 54)
- **3 Mar 1992:** Chairman Izetbegovic declared independence of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina.
- **6 Apr 1992:** US and European Community recognized BiH as an independent state.
- **22 May 1992:** BiH admitted to the United Nations. (Karadzic Judgment, Para. 56)

### The Platform Itself

The Bosnian Presidency's platform was built on three pillars:

1. **Sovereign, integral Bosnia-Herzegovina** within internationally recognized borders
2. **Multi-ethnic civic state** -- a state of all its citizens regardless of ethnicity
3. **Opposition to ethnic partition** -- rejection of any division along ethnic lines

Bosnia was "unique in that, unlike the other republics, it possessed no single majority ethnic grouping" within Yugoslavia (Karadzic Judgment, Para. 32). No recognized distinct "Bosnian nation" existed before the conflict. The 1991 census showed approximately 44% Muslim/Bosniak, 31% Serb, 17% Croat, and 8% Yugoslav/Other.

The Presidency's position was that this diversity required a civic rather than ethnic model of statehood.

### The Lisbon Conference Context (Feb-Mar 1992)

The Cutileiro Plan (Lisbon Conference) proposed dividing Bosnia into three constituent ethnic units with a weak central government. On 18 March 1992, all three sides initially signed: Izetbegovic for Bosniaks, Karadzic for Serbs, Boban for Croats.

On 28 March 1992, Izetbegovic withdrew his signature after meeting with US Ambassador Warren Zimmermann, declaring opposition to ethnic division of Bosnia. Former UK Foreign Secretary Lord Carrington stated the US encouraged Izetbegovic to walk away. Zimmermann reportedly conveyed assurances of US recognition for Bosnian independence without internal ethnic compromises.

This withdrawal is the decisive moment where the civic multi-ethnic platform became the war aim. It meant rejecting ethnic cantonization in favor of a unitary state.

## 2. ARBiH Ethnic Composition

### Early War (1992)

Chief of Staff Sefer Halilovic claimed in June 1992 that ARBiH forces were:
- **70% Muslim (Bosniak)**
- **18% Croat**
- **12% Serb**

Initially, approximately 25% of the ARBiH was composed of non-Bosniaks. The percentage of Serb and Croat soldiers was particularly high in Sarajevo, Mostar, and Tuzla -- the most urbanized, multi-ethnic areas.

### Decline Over Time

The multi-ethnic composition eroded rapidly. By mid-war, out of the initial 18% Croat and 12% Serb contingent, only approximately 1% of each remained in the ranks. The reasons:

1. **Croat-Bosniak War (1993):** HVO-ARBiH conflict drove most Croats out of ARBiH
2. **Serb self-selection:** Most Serbs who would serve in ARBiH were urban liberals concentrated in Sarajevo and Tuzla; their numbers were finite
3. **Ethnic cleansing:** Serb-held territory was largely inaccessible, limiting Serb recruitment to government-controlled areas

### Notable Non-Bosniak Officers

- **Jovan Divjak (Serb):** Deputy Commander of ARBiH, the most senior Serb officer. Defended Sarajevo. He described his role candidly: "Of course, someone has to be a flower arrangement too" -- acknowledging the symbolic nature of non-Bosniak representation at senior levels.
- **Stjepan Siber (Croat):** Deputy Commander of ARBiH General Staff. He and Divjak were the only non-Bosniaks on the general staff.

### Significance for the Game Decision

The ethnic composition data reveals the tension at the heart of the civic state position:
- **The aspiration was genuine:** 30% non-Bosniak composition in mid-1992 is remarkable for a wartime army
- **The erosion was structural:** Not driven by policy choice but by the war itself (the Croat-Bosniak conflict, territorial fragmentation)
- **The symbolism mattered internationally:** Non-Bosniak officers were essential for the legitimacy of the civic state claim

## 3. The Civic Republic Position in Practice

### What It Actually Meant

The civic state position held that:
- Bosnia's sovereignty derived from its citizens, not from its constituent peoples
- Territorial integrity was non-negotiable -- no partition, no secession
- All citizens had equal rights regardless of ethnicity
- The state's army defended all Bosnians, not just Bosniaks
- Peace plans that divided Bosnia along ethnic lines were unacceptable

### How It Played Out

In practice, the civic position provided:

**Diplomatic leverage:**
- Strongest card with the international community, which recognized Bosnia as a sovereign, integral state
- Basis for UN membership and international legal standing
- Moral authority when opposing partition plans (Vance-Owen, Owen-Stoltenberg, etc.)

**Military implications:**
- Justified maintaining a single, nationally integrated command structure
- Provided legal basis for authority over all territory within recognized borders
- Enabled recruitment beyond Bosniak communities (critical in early 1992)

**Limitations:**
- Increasingly at odds with reality as ethnic separation proceeded on the ground
- The Bosniak-dominated character of the army and government became more pronounced over time
- International mediators repeatedly proposed ethnic partition anyway (Lisbon, Vance-Owen, Owen-Stoltenberg, Contact Group)
- The final Dayton settlement (1995) effectively accepted ethnic division with two entities

## 4. The Bosniak Nationalist Alternative

### The Islamic Declaration Debate

Izetbegovic's 1970 essay "The Islamic Declaration" was weaponized by Serb and Croat nationalists as evidence that the SDA sought an Islamic state. Key facts:

- Written in 1970, published officially in Sarajevo in 1990
- Addressed the Islamic world broadly, not Bosnia specifically
- Izetbegovic stated repeatedly that the Declaration was hypothetical and "not to be applied to the situation in Bosnia"
- He described himself as supporting a multi-ethnic country and practiced a liberal form of Islam

### Who Advocated It

A more explicitly Bosniak-national position was never the mainstream SDA platform, but elements existed:
- Hardliners within SDA who saw the civic position as naive given Serb and Croat ethno-nationalism
- Military commanders who after 1993 viewed the multi-ethnic framing as a fiction
- The 1993 adoption of "Bosniak" as the ethnic name (replacing "Muslim") at the 1st Congress of Bosniak-Muslim Intellectuals signaled a crystallizing national identity

### What Would Have Changed

A Bosniak national state framework would have meant:
- **Army:** Explicitly Bosniak army rather than multi-ethnic facade; non-Bosniak officers sidelined
- **Diplomacy:** Weaker international position; harder to argue against ethnic partition if you accept ethnic logic
- **Recruitment:** Potentially stronger internal cohesion but smaller recruitment base
- **Alliances:** Impossible to maintain the fiction of Bosniak-Croat cooperation; faster break with HVO
- **Post-war:** No basis for opposing ethnic partition at Dayton; likely even more unfavorable terms

## 5. International Community Reactions

### To the Civic Position

The international community formally endorsed the civic state framework:
- Recognized Bosnia within its internationally recognized borders
- Repeatedly affirmed sovereignty and territorial integrity in UN resolutions
- Arms embargo applied to "Yugoslavia" (effectively all parties), treating Bosnia as part of a unified entity

But in practice, the international community was ambivalent:
- Every peace plan from Lisbon (Feb 1992) through Dayton (Nov 1995) proposed some form of ethnic division
- The US was most supportive of the unitary state vision (Zimmermann's encouragement to reject Lisbon)
- European mediators (Carrington, Owen, Stoltenberg) were more willing to accept ethnic realities on the ground
- The final Dayton Agreement created a state of two entities that was formally unified but practically divided

### To the Nationalist Alternative

Had the Bosnian government adopted an explicitly Bosniak national framework:
- International support would have eroded -- the "multi-ethnic victim" narrative was Bosnia's strongest card
- Arms embargo opponents (who argued Bosnia had a right to self-defense) would have lost their moral argument
- Serb and Croat claims to ethnic self-determination would have been legitimized
- International mediators would have had an easier time imposing a three-way partition

## 6. Game Decision Framework

### Option A: Civic Multi-Ethnic State (Historical Path)

**Modifiers:**
- International legitimacy bonus (diplomatic leverage)
- Broader initial recruitment pool (+18% Croat, +12% Serb in early war)
- Slower loss of those recruits if Croat-Bosniak war can be delayed
- Vulnerability: gap between rhetoric and reality damages morale when non-Bosniak soldiers leave
- Post-war: stronger negotiating position at peace table

### Option B: Bosniak National State

**Modifiers:**
- Internal cohesion bonus (no pretense; clearer identity)
- Smaller recruitment base (Bosniak only from the start)
- International legitimacy penalty
- Accelerates Croat-Bosniak conflict (HVO breaks immediately)
- Opens door to Islamic world support (mujahideen, arms from Iran/Saudi Arabia) more openly
- Post-war: weaker position; ethnic partition more likely

### Option C: Pragmatic Hybrid

**Modifiers:**
- Civic rhetoric externally, Bosniak mobilization internally
- Maintains international support while building national cohesion
- Risk: if exposed, damages credibility with both audiences
- Historical reality: this is closest to what actually happened by 1993-1994
- Moderate bonuses across the board, no major penalties
