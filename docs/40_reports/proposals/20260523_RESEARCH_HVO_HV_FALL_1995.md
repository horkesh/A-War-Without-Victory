# Research: HV and HVO Operations in Fall 1995

**Date:** 2026-05-23
**Author:** Research dispatch (historian-style synthesis)
**Scope:** Western Bosnia, July–October 1995. The HV (Hrvatska vojska — Croatian Army proper) and HVO (Hrvatsko vijeće obrane — Croatian Defence Council, Bosnian Croat army) operations that, jointly with ARBiH, broke VRS resistance and forced Republika Srpska to Dayton.
**Source hierarchy applied:** ICTY verdicts (Gotovina & Markač, Prlić et al.) > Balkan Battlegrounds (CIA, 2002, as cited via secondary literature) > Wikipedia (cross-checked) > regional/press sources.

This document is **research only**. It does not propose engine fixes. A short "engine representability gap" note appears at the end.

---

## 1. Strategic context — why fall 1995 broke the war

Between late July and mid-October 1995 five operations — **Summer '95, Storm, Mistral 2 (Maestral), Sana, Una, Southern Move** — together collapsed the VRS line across roughly 4,000–5,000 km² of western Bosnia, lifted the three-year siege of Bihać, intercepted the RSK's logistical spine, broke the VRS 2nd Krajina Corps as a coherent formation, brought ARBiH and HV/HVO to within ~25 miles of Banja Luka, and forced Milošević to negotiate at Dayton on a 51:49 territorial split.

The legal scaffolding for HV intervention in BiH was the **Split Agreement of 22 July 1995**, signed by Tuđman, Izetbegović, Krešimir Zubak (Federation president) and Haris Silajdžić (BiH PM). It explicitly invited the HV into BiH "to assist in relieving the siege of Bihać" and to "coordinate military activities" — the legal cover under which a Croatian standing army openly operated on Bosnian territory for the next eleven weeks.

The hidden scaffolding — established earlier — is the ICTY **Prlić et al.** finding (Trial Chamber III, 29 May 2013; Appeals Chamber, November 2017): from mid-January 1993 onward, Croatia "exercised overall control" over the HVO, "released Croatian Army officers to command HVO forces," and provided logistical support. By summer 1995 the HV–HVO command relationship was not "two allied armies coordinating" — it was a single Zagreb-directed force structure with Ante Gotovina (HV major general) commanding combined HV+HVO operations on BiH soil with full Federation political consent.

## 2. Operation Summer '95 (Ljeto '95) — 25–29 July 1995

The pre-Storm shaping operation. Not in the original research target list, but it is structurally inseparable from the question "did Oluja affect Bosnia?": Summer '95 captured the BiH territory from which Storm was launched.

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Dates | 25–29 July 1995 |
| Theatre | Livanjsko polje, Bosansko Grahovo, Glamoč (western BiH) |
| HV/HVO commander | Lt Gen Ante Gotovina (HV Split MD) |
| Attacking force | ~8,500 in main assault; ~25,000 HV+HVO total in operation |
| VRS opposing | VRS 2nd Krajina Corps, ~5,500 |
| Territory captured | ~1,600 km² |
| Strategic result | Cut the Knin–Drvar road (RSK's main lateral supply route); positioned HV to attack Knin from the south; forced RSK to redeploy reserves away from Bihać |

This is the operation that converted Bihać's three-year siege from "geographically unrelievable" to "one corps movement away from relief."

## 3. Operation Storm (Oluja) — Bosnian-theatre spillover, 4–7 August 1995

Storm itself is a Croatian internal operation against the Republika Srpska Krajina (RSK). The user's request is the *spillover*. Five effects:

### 3.1 Direct lifting of the Bihać siege
The ARBiH 5th Corps under Divisional General Atif Dudaković attacked outward from the Bihać pocket simultaneously with Storm, engaging ARSK forces from the rear. Approximately 3,000 ARBiH troops participated in the pincer. By the time Storm formally closed on 7 August (mop-up to 14 August), the Bihać pocket — besieged in some form since 1992 — was permanently relieved. Total siege casualties (1991–1995) across Bihać, Bosanska Krupa, Cazin and Velika Kladuša: 4,856–5,000 killed or missing.

### 3.2 ARBiH 5th Corps territorial recovery into RSK territory
The 5th Corps liberated parts of Croatian territory around Donji Lapac, Plitvice and Dvor na Uni — the only documented case in the war of an ARBiH formation operating offensively outside BiH's borders.

### 3.3 VRS 2nd Krajina Corps neutralisation
On the night of 11/12 August the VRS 2nd Krajina Corps launched its only significant counter-attack, breaking through the HV 141st Reserve Infantry Brigade and reaching the outskirts of Bosansko Grahovo. The HV repulsed it with one battalion each drawn from the 4th and 7th Guards Brigades plus the 6th and 126th Home Guard Regiments. The corps was not destroyed, but it was structurally exhausted and short of reserves — a precondition for its collapse five weeks later under Mistral 2.

### 3.4 VRS strategic-reserve absence
Wikipedia (citing BB) explicitly notes: "The RSK relied on the Republika Srpska and Yugoslav militaries as its strategic reserve, but they did not intervene." The VRS chose not to commit forces to defend the Krajina — a choice that left the VRS 2nd Krajina Corps fighting alone and that, post-Storm, left no VRS operational reserve available to backstop the Mistral 2 and Sana axes.

### 3.5 Refugee column and demographic shock
150,000–200,000 Krajina Serbs fled into Bosnia and Serbia. A column was struck near Bosanski Petrovac on 8 August (separate Wikipedia entry). The demographic shock — large numbers of dispossessed Krajina Serb fighters mixed into the VRS rear — degraded VRS 2nd Krajina Corps morale and absorbed logistical capacity that should have gone to defending western BiH.

## 4. Operation Mistral 2 / Maestral — 8–15 September 1995

The relationship the user asked to clarify: **Mistral and Maestral are the same operation** — "Maestral" is the original Croatian codename (a Mediterranean wind), "Mistral 2" is the international/Anglophone rendering. Some sources call it "Operation Mistral" without the "2"; the "2" distinguishes it from a separate earlier 1995 minor action. **There is no separate "Operation Maestral" preceding it.**

### 4.1 Forces — combined HV/HVO

Three Operational Groups under overall command of HV Major General Ante Gotovina.

| OG | Commander | Composition | Mission |
|---|---|---|---|
| **OG North** (main effort, ~11,000) | Gotovina (direct) | HV: 4th Guards Bde, 7th Guards Bde, 1st Croatian Guards Bde (1st HGZ); HVO: 1st, 2nd, 3rd Guards Bdes; 60th Guards Bn, GS Recce-Sabotage Coy, special police | Capture Šipovo and Jajce |
| **OG South** (reserve/flank) | Brig Ante Kotromanović | HV: 6th HGR, 126th HGR, 141st Inf Bde | Pin VRS forces, flank security |
| **OG West** (supporting) | Brig Mladen Fuzul | HV: 7th, 15th, 134th Home Guard Regiments; 112th, 113th Inf Bdes | Drvar axis |

Note the **structural fact** the ICTY Prlić judgment confirms: the three HVO "Guards Brigades" in OG North are operating *inside* an HV operational-group structure under an HV major general's direct command. There is no separate HVO main staff axis in Mistral 2 — the HVO contribution is plugged into HV command at the brigade level.

### 4.2 VRS opposing

- VRS 2nd Krajina Corps (Maj Gen Radivoje Tomanić)
- VRS 30th Infantry Division of 1st Krajina Corps (Maj Gen Momir Zec)
- Total nominal strength ~22,000, of which only ~5,000–6,000 actually deployed against the HV assault on opening day. One armoured battalion in reserve.

The 22,000-vs-11,000 paper ratio is misleading: the VRS was over-extended along an arc from Drvar to Donji Vakuf with no operational reserve, and the deployable-at-point-of-contact ratio favoured the HV/HVO ~2:1 on opening day.

### 4.3 Sequence

- **8 September:** Main attack launched. OG North breaches VRS defences north of Glamoč.
- **12 September:** Second-stage offensive begins.
- **13 September:** Jajce falls — the symbolic capture (medieval Bosnian-Croat royal capital).
- **15 September:** Šipovo and Drvar fall. Operation closes.

### 4.4 Results

- ~2,500 km² captured.
- Up to 30 km penetration.
- HV/HVO: 74 KIA, 226 WIA.
- VRS losses "likely far greater" (no precise tribunal figure; BB estimates substantial, including loss of corps-level coherence).
- VRS 2nd Krajina Corps no longer functioned as a coherent corps after 15 September.

### 4.5 ARBiH coordination

Mistral 2 was **synchronised** with the ARBiH Operation Sana (see §5). On the HV right flank, the ARBiH 7th Corps advanced from Travnik/Bugojno; on the HV left flank further west, the ARBiH 5th Corps pushed from Bihać. The forces linked at **Oštrelj Pass** between Bosanski Petrovac (5th Corps) and Drvar (HV OG West). This is the only documented operational-level junction between ARBiH and HV ground forces of the entire war.

## 5. ARBiH Operation Sana — 13 September – 12 October 1995

The HV-coordinated ARBiH offensive on the inboard (eastern) flank of Mistral 2 / Southern Move. Concurrent and inseparable.

### 5.1 Forces

**5th Corps** (15,000) under Div Gen Atif Dudaković, in two OGs:
- OG South: 501st Mountain, 502nd Mountain, 510th Liberation, 517th Light brigades
- OG North: 503rd Mountain, 505th Mountain, 506th Liberation, 511th Mountain brigades

**7th Corps reinforcement** (~10,000 transferred mid-op): Guards Brigade, 17th Krajina Mountain, 717th Mountain, 708th Light, 712th Mountain.

The 7th Corps transfer is operationally significant — ARBiH stripped its central-Bosnia front to reinforce the western push once Mistral 2 had ruptured VRS lines.

### 5.2 Sequence

- **13 Sep:** Launch from Bihać.
- **15 Sep:** Bosanski Petrovac taken.
- **17 Sep:** Ključ and Bosanska Krupa taken.
- **18–19 Sep:** Encounter with 14,000 VRS reinforcements near Novi Grad/Sanski Most.
- **23 Sep – 3 Oct:** VRS counter-offensive pushes ARBiH back ~6 km, threatens 5th Corps with encirclement around Ključ.
- **8–11 Oct:** HV/HVO Operation Southern Move (see §6) relieves Ključ pressure.
- **12 Oct:** Sanski Most captured the same day a comprehensive ceasefire takes effect.

### 5.3 Results

- 70 km initial advance.
- ARBiH: 178 KIA, 588 WIA, 41 captured.
- VRS: ~900 KIA, ~1,000 WIA.
- 30,000–50,000 Serb civilians displaced from captured territory.

### 5.4 The Ključ crisis and HV rescue

The cardinal fact about Sana is that the ARBiH **requested HV assistance** when 5th Corps was threatened around Ključ. The HV and HVO agreed; Operation Southern Move (§6) was the resulting relief operation. This is the only documented case in the war of ARBiH formally requesting and receiving HV ground-force intervention to rescue a Bosnian corps from imminent operational defeat.

## 6. Operation Una — 18–19 September 1995

A separate, failed HV-only river-crossing offensive worth noting because it shows the operational ceiling. Planned hastily after the Tuđman–Holbrooke meeting of 14 September in Zagreb (Holbrooke pressing for additional pressure on VRS to relieve Sarajevo).

- HV 1st and 2nd Guards Brigades (~1,500 troops), Zagreb Corps.
- Crossings attempted at Novi Grad, Bosanska Dubica, Bosanska Kostajnica, Jasenovac.
- Small bridgeheads established, pinned by VRS artillery and MGs, called off after two days.
- HV losses: ~50 KIA, ~180 WIA.
- Outcome: complete failure, no territorial gain.

Una is the negative control case — it shows what happens when an HV operation is hastily planned without HVO ground-force coordination and against a prepared VRS defence. Compare to Mistral 2 / Southern Move where HV+HVO coordination and VRS over-extension produced one-sided results.

## 7. Operation Southern Move (Južni potez) — 8–11 October 1995

The final operation. Triggered by the 5th Corps Ključ crisis (§5.4).

### 7.1 Forces

| Side | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| HV/HVO | 11,000–12,000 in two groups, Maj Gen Ante Gotovina | 4th Guards Brigade central role |
| VRS | ~5,500 in three brigades | 7th Motorised Brigade in path of main effort |

### 7.2 Objectives

- Capture Mrkonjić Grad (transit chokepoint south-west of Banja Luka).
- Seize Manjača Mountain (artillery dominance over Banja Luka).
- Capture Bočac Hydroelectric Power Station — "the last significant source of electricity under VRS control in western BiH."
- Relieve 5th Corps pressure at Ključ.

### 7.3 Sequence and result

- **8 Oct:** Offensive launched. US Brigadier (later President of Croatia) Ante Kotromanović — then commander of 126th HGR — later stated the offensive was "approved by the United States" and that "U.S. warplanes attacked VRS positions near Banja Luka in preparation."
- **10 Oct:** 4th Guards Brigade breaks through and outflanks VRS 7th Motorised Brigade; VRS withdraws from Mrkonjić Grad.
- **11 Oct:** Operation closes.
- **12 Oct:** Country-wide ceasefire takes effect (one day after operation ends — the timing is not coincidental).

VRS casualties (Bosnian Serb sources): 480 dead or missing; 181 bodies recovered from a mass grave at Mrkonjić Grad cemetery (subsequent atrocity charges fall outside this brief).

### 7.4 Why it stopped before Banja Luka — primary sources

The "halted by US pressure" framing is partly correct but understates Tuđman's own agency. Two documented data points:

1. **UPI Archives, 19 September 1995 (Zagreb meeting, Tuđman–Izetbegović–Holbrooke):** "Muslim-led Bosnian army forces positioned barely 25 miles from Banja Luka" agreed to halt offensive operations following Holbrooke's intervention.

2. **Tuđman to Izetbegović, Split, 19 September (cited via Balkan Insight / BB-derived):** *"Through our military actions, we have almost reached the 51:49 formula, and now we must accept the cessation of offensive operations and accept political dialogue. If we continue the offensive toward Banja Luka, there will be no peace process."*

The Banja Luka halt is therefore best read as **two converging incentives**: US diplomatic pressure (Holbrooke) plus Tuđman's strategic judgment that exceeding 51:49 would derail Dayton. There was no "stopped by the US against Croatian wishes" moment; Tuđman accepted the constraint because he had already won everything he wanted from the operation.

General Wesley Clark stated post-war that a stronger Dayton agreement would have been possible had ARBiH/HV been allowed to take Banja Luka — a counterfactual judgment, not a documented decision-point.

## 8. HVO independent operations (without HV)

The user asked about HVO actions independent of HV in fall 1995. **There are essentially none of operational significance.** The HVO's 1995 contribution was:

1. **HVO Guards Brigades (1st, 2nd, 3rd) embedded into HV OG North** under Gotovina's direct command — Mistral 2 and Southern Move. Not "HVO operating alongside HV"; HVO brigades operationally subordinate to HV.

2. **Central Bosnia HVO enclaves** (Vitez, Kiseljak, Žepče, Vareš, Usora) — remained isolated throughout 1995, did not participate in the western push. Vitez and Kiseljak in particular stayed cut off; the Washington Agreement of March 1994 had ended HVO-ARBiH combat but did not topologically reconnect these enclaves. They became HVO "islands" of mixed Federation administration.

3. **Western Herzegovina HVO consolidation** — the south-western HVO formations (1st, 2nd, 3rd Guards Brigades, drawn from western Herzegovina) were exactly the units mobilised north for Mistral 2 / Southern Move. There was no independent western Herzegovina offensive; the HVO emptied its strongest formations into Gotovina's operational groups.

4. **HVO coordination with ARBiH 7th Corps:** the Federation military framework (post-Washington 1994) included Joint Command Council mechanisms, but in operational practice fall 1995 the ARBiH 7th Corps fought Sana eastward from Bugojno/Travnik while HVO Guards Brigades fought Mistral 2 westward — they were *adjacent* on the same map but operating under different commanders. The "linkage" at Oštrelj Pass was 5th Corps with HV OG West, not 7th Corps with HVO.

So: in fall 1995 the HVO is effectively an HV-controlled brigade pool, plus a set of isolated central-Bosnia enclaves with no offensive role.

## 9. HV cross-border doctrine — how HV operated inside BiH

Three legal/operational layers, working backward from observed reality:

### 9.1 Layer 1 — pre-Split Agreement disguise (1992–early 1995)
ICTY Prlić et al. (Trial Chamber 2013, Appeals 2017) found Croatia exercised "overall control" over the HVO from 1993 onward — meaning HV officers were transferred to command HVO units, HV logistics flowed to HVO formations, and HV brigades occasionally operated in BiH under HVO designation. Public-facing fiction: "the HVO is a Bosnian Croat army." Legal reality: it was a Croatian-controlled paramilitary in BiH. This is the basis for ICTY's finding of an *international* armed conflict in central Bosnia 1993–1994 (rather than internal civil war).

### 9.2 Layer 2 — Split Agreement open intervention (22 July – Dayton, 1995)
After 22 July 1995 the HV operated *openly* in BiH with full Bosnian government consent. No disguise was needed. Gotovina commanded combined HV+HVO formations under HV insignia. This is the layer at which Summer '95, Mistral 2, Una, and Southern Move occurred.

### 9.3 Layer 3 — operational command unification
By Mistral 2 the HV and HVO were a single operational structure: an HV major general (Gotovina), HV operational groups (North/South/West), HVO Guards Brigades embedded as line units inside an HV OG. There was no parallel HVO main staff axis. This is the "joint" structure the user asked about: not two armies coordinating, one army with two recruitment streams (Croatian conscripts to HV brigades; Bosnian Croat conscripts to HVO Guards Brigades) feeding one operational chain of command.

**The HV did not need to disguise as HVO in fall 1995 — the Split Agreement made it legal — but HVO brigades effectively functioned as line units of the HV.**

## 10. ICTY findings on joint command

Two convictions are directly relevant.

### 10.1 Gotovina & Markač (2011 trial conviction, 2012 appeal acquittal)
Trial Chamber convicted on joint criminal enterprise (JCE) findings — to permanently remove Serb civilian population from Krajina via Operation Storm. JCE members listed in the indictment included Tuđman (President), Šušak (Defence Minister), Bobetko (HV chief of staff to July 1995), and Červenko (HV chief of staff from July 1995). Appeals Chamber overturned on procedural grounds (artillery-impact analysis), not on rebutting the command-coordination findings. **For the engine question — who commanded what — the Trial Chamber findings on HV command structure remain authoritative.**

### 10.2 Prlić et al. (2013 trial, 2017 final appeal)
This is the structurally important judgment for the HVO question. Findings:
- Croatia exercised "overall control" over the HVO from January 1993.
- HV officers were "released" to command HVO forces.
- HV logistical support flowed to HVO.
- HV units entered BiH and "directly participate[d] in the conflict."
- The HVO was part of a JCE from January 1993 to April 1994 aimed at creating a Croatian entity in BiH via ethnic cleansing of Muslims.

The Prlić finding is the definitive legal answer to "was the HVO an independent Bosnian Croat army or a Croatian proxy?" — it was a Croatian proxy with significant local recruitment, command-controlled from Zagreb, and treated as such by international humanitarian law.

## 12. Engine representability gap — what the current model likely cannot do

The user explicitly asked for callouts, not solutions. Five gaps follow directly from the historical record:

### 12.1 HV as a non-modeled faction providing decisive support
The current model has factions RBiH, RS, HRHB. The HV is not a separate faction. But in fall 1995 the HV provided:
- 8,500 troops in Summer '95 (out of ~25,000 HV+HVO total).
- ~11,000 troops in OG North of Mistral 2 (plus ~5,000 in OG South and OG West).
- ~1,500 in Una.
- ~11,000–12,000 in Southern Move.
- Plus operational command (Gotovina, an HV major general).

If the model treats HRHB = HVO and folds HV contribution into HVO order of battle, then HVO strength is over-stated and HVO political agency is over-stated — because the HV brigades were commanded from Zagreb, not from Mostar/Grude. The Mistral 2 / Southern Move force could not have been generated by HVO alone; HVO Guards Brigades were ~3 of ~9 brigades in OG North.

### 12.2 Cross-border operations
The current sim is, by construction, *inside* BiH. Storm's territory captures were in Croatia; the BiH spillover (Bihać relief, 2nd Krajina Corps degradation, refugee column) was a *consequence* of an external-to-BiH operation. The sim cannot represent "an operation outside the map whose effects propagate inside the map." Bihać relief in particular is impossible to generate endogenously from BiH-internal mechanics — it required HV operations in Croatia to cut the RSK in half.

### 12.3 Joint command at the corps level
Mistral 2 placed HVO Guards Brigades under HV OG North under an HV major general. The current commander hierarchy presumably has HRHB brigades reporting up to HRHB main staff, RS brigades up to VRS main staff, RBiH brigades up to ARBiH main staff. There is no provision for "this HRHB brigade is operationally subordinate to an unmodeled external commander." The Split Agreement structurally rewrote command-and-control for the second half of 1995 in a way the engine can't model without an HV faction or an HV virtual commander.

### 12.4 ARBiH–HV–HVO operational synchronisation
Sana, Mistral 2, and Southern Move were *coordinated*. Sana's 5th Corps attacked because Mistral 2 had ruptured VRS lines on its flank. Southern Move was launched specifically to relieve Sana's 5th Corps at Ključ. This is a tri-faction synchronous offensive across one operational theatre — the kind of behaviour that emerges only if (a) all three forces share an objective layer and (b) the engine can model formal coalition operations rather than ad-hoc parallel attacks.

### 12.5 Externally-imposed stopping conditions (Banja Luka halt)
Southern Move stopped on 11 October because (a) Tuđman accepted the 51:49 formula and (b) Holbrooke pressed for a halt. Neither input is generated by the sim's own combat or political mechanics — both come from outside the model: from external diplomacy (Holbrooke) and from a coalition partner's strategic calculation about a peace process the sim itself does not yet contain. Without a Dayton-anticipation mechanism, the engine has no reason to stop a successful offensive.

### 12.6 Operation Una as negative control
Una is worth noting because it shows the engine should *not* always make HV interventions succeed. Una was hasty, river-crossing, no HVO coordination, prepared VRS defence — and it failed in 48 hours. If an engine surfaces HV intervention as "magic 1995 reinforcement," it will over-represent HV effectiveness. Mistral 2 worked because of VRS over-extension and HV+HVO concentration; Una failed because neither of those conditions held.
