## 0. Executive Summary

Between the Washington Agreement (18 March 1994) and the fall of Knin (5 August 1995), the ARBiH conducted **seven major offensive campaigns** and **two defensive crises**, almost none of which produced the strategic breakthroughs their planners hoped for. The year is often skipped over in popular narratives — sandwiched between the Croat-Bosniak war that ended in March 1994 and the dramatic August–October 1995 cascade. But operationally it was decisive: 1994 is the year the ARBiH learned what it could not do with light infantry alone against dug-in VRS positions, and the year the institutional foundations were laid for the 1995 corps-and-division offensives. The pattern repeats relentlessly:

> **ARBiH attacks. Initial surprise, 1–5 km gains. VRS recovers, lateral reinforces from 1KK or borrows armor from corps troops, brings artillery to bear. ARBiH casualties high, position consolidated short of objective. VRS counter-attacks recapture marginal ground but cannot dislodge the new ARBiH line. Both sides dig in for winter.**

The seven 1994 offensive lanes:

| # | Op (or campaign) | Faction | Dates | Area | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5th Corps Operations vs APZB | ARBiH 5th C | Jan – Aug 1994 | Cazinska Krajina (Velika Kladuša) | Abdić defeated August, fled briefly |
| 2 | "Just Out of Reach" — Donji Vakuf | ARBiH 7th C | Mar – Nov 1994 | Bugojno–Donji Vakuf axis | ~100 km² gained, town not taken |
| 3 | Mt. Vlašić assaults | ARBiH 7th C + 3rd C | Mar – Sep 1994 | Vlašić plateau | Some perimeter gains, summit not taken |
| 4 | Tešanj–Teslić offensive | ARBiH 3rd C (OG 7-South) | Mar – Sep 1994 | Tešanj enclave westward | Limited high ground, halt 5–6 km E of Teslić |
| 5 | VRS *Breza-94* (defensive) | VRS 1KK+2KK+SVK vs ARBiH 5th C | 31 Aug – 15 Sep 1994 | Bihać pocket | VRS attack defeated; 5th Corps counter-pursuit |
| 6 | Operation *Grmeč 94* (the breakout) | ARBiH 5th C | 25 Oct – 3 Nov 1994 | Grabež plateau → Kulen Vakuf/Bos. Krupa | ~250 km² gained, then partially lost |
| 7 | HVO *Cincar* / ARBiH Kupres axis | ARBiH 7th C + HVO | 20 Oct – 3 Nov 1994 | Kupres plateau | Kupres town captured 3 Nov; ~600 km² |

And the two defensive crises:

| # | Defensive op | Faction | Dates | Area | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | VRS *Štit-94* (Shield) — the Bihać crisis | ARBiH 5th C vs VRS Composite Tactical Groups | 4 Nov 1994 – Feb 1995 | Bihać pocket | ~150 km² recovered by VRS; pocket survives |
| D2 | OG *Pauk* (Spider) + APWB return | ARBiH 5th C vs RSK/VJ/APZB/RDB | Nov 1994 – Aug 1995 | NW Bihać pocket | Abdić reinstalled Kladuša Dec 1994; APWB returns |

Plus the early 1995 VRS offensive activity in central Bosnia and the ARBiH 1st Corps spring–summer 1995 Sarajevo theatre actions (Trnovo / Treskavica) that overlap with the larger calendar but precede *Sana 95*.

**Key takeaway:** The ARBiH that launched *Sana 95* in September 1995 was not a new force. It was a force that had **lost a year of grinding attempts** against the same VRS positions (Donji Vakuf, Vlašić, Teslić) without taking any of them. The October 1994 breakout in Bihać had been recovered by the VRS within six weeks. What changed in summer 1995 was not the ARBiH — it was the *exogenous collapse of the SVK and the arrival of NATO/RRF/HV* enabling forces. The autumn 1995 success required the ARBiH to be ready; 1994 made it ready, but 1994 also proved it could not break out alone.

## 1. Operation *Grmeč 94* and the Bihać Crisis (October 1994 – February 1995)

This is the single largest 1994 operational episode and the precondition for understanding 5th Corps state in 1995. Covered in detail in BB2 Annex 61 (pp. 526–541). The companion VRS 2KK collapse doc (`20260523_RESEARCH_VRS_2KK_COLLAPSE.md`) describes the same period from the Bosnian Serb side — this section is the ARBiH side.

### 1.1 Force composition — ARBiH 5th Corps, October 1994

BB2 p.541 puts the 5th Corps at **~15,000 troops** committed to the Grmeč battle. Order of battle (BB2 p.541, cross-checked with BB2 Annex 60 and Annex 61):

| Brigade | Role in offensive | Axis |
|---|---|---|
| 501st Bihać Mountain | spearhead | S toward Ripač/Orašac/Kulen Vakuf/Bos. Petrovac |
| 502nd Bihać Mountain | spearhead | S toward Ripač/Orašac (with 501st) |
| 503rd Cazin Mountain | left-prong | E along Una toward Bos. Krupa |
| 511th Bos. Krupa Mountain | left-prong | E along Una, then river crossing N of Krupa |
| 1st Bosnian Liberation | left-prong support | E along Una (with 503rd, 511th) |
| 505th Buzim Motorized | reserve / counter-attack | northern axis (post-Breza-94 victors) |
| 517th Light | reserve | central |

Corps-level enablers: limited artillery (the 5th Corps had captured 122mm D-30 howitzers, a few 130mm M-46s, BM-21 captured from APZB), a few tanks (10 T-55/M-84 estimated, mostly captured from Abdić's APZB and SVK forays into the pocket), the elite Hamza/Gazije recon-sabotage companies, the Tajfun battalion (505th brigade attached).

Opposing VRS forces — see VRS 2KK collapse doc §1 for full detail. Summary: 4 light infantry brigades on the Grabež (1st Drvar, 3rd Petrovac, 15th Bihać, 17th Ključ) totaling ~5,000–5,500 (BB2 p.540).

### 1.2 Axes and outcome

| Axis | Lead unit(s) | Target | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Southern | 501st, 502nd Mtn Bdes | Ripač → Orašac → Kulen Vakuf → Bos. Petrovac (~40 km) | Reached Kulen Vakuf 28 Oct; held briefly; abandoned 3–4 Nov as VRS *Štit-94* built up |
| Eastern | 503rd, 511th Mtn, 1st Lib | Una valley → Bos. Krupa (~20 km) | Encircled Krupa 29 Oct; town not taken; high-water mark |
| Northern flank | 505th Mot | Defensive screen | Held against APWB/SVK probes |

**Day 1–2 (25–27 October):** Surprise achieved. Grabež barracks taken, Grabež plateau cleared. ~100–150 km² captured (BB2 p.527). Heavy equipment captured and immediately recycled into ARBiH inventory. 3,500 Serb civilians initially fled, eventually ~10,000 (BB2 p.527).

**Days 3–5 (28–30 October):** Karadžić declares "state of war" in 2KK area (29 Oct), suspending leaves, ordering full mobilisation, restricting civilian movement to prevent depopulation (BB2 p.547). ARBiH advance reaches Kulen Vakuf (~30 km SE of Bihać) and the outskirts of Bos. Krupa. Total captured ~250 km². But the advance is outpacing logistics.

**Days 6–9 (31 Oct – 3 Nov):** ARBiH momentum exhausted. The 511th and 505th try river crossings around Krupa and are repulsed by the 11th Krupa Light Infantry Brigade — which is trapped in the town's east-bank half but cannot be dislodged. VRS Maj. Gen. Rajko Balac arrives with a composite battalion drawn from 6th Sanski Infantry and 43rd Prijedor Motorized (from 1KK) plus a Banja Luka cadet battalion. Balac's scratch force hits the 5th Corps right flank SW of Krupa on 30 October, halting it (BB2 p.547).

### 1.3 The Štit-94 counteroffensive (4 November – ~20 November 1994)

Operation *Štit-94* (Shield-94) was VRS Main Staff chief **Lt. Gen. Manojlo Milovanović's** personal counteroffensive. He arrived at Krupa on 30 October, established his command post in Jasenica (~13 km SE of Bos. Krupa), and assumed command of "all VRS formations in the Bihać theatre" (BB2 p.548).

**VRS force assembled:** initial 6,500, grew to ~14,000 by mid-November (BB2 p.548). Three composite "Serbian Brigades" formed from elements drawn from every VRS corps except Herzegovina Corps (BB2 p.559, fn 21):
- **1st Serbian Brigade** (Balac, KIA 4 Nov) — Schools battalion + 1 bn each from 43rd Prijedor Mot, 6th Sanski, composite Drina Corps battalion, major elements of 3rd Petrovac Light.
- **2nd Serbian Brigade** — 3 composite battalions; major elements 1st Drvar Light.
- **3rd Serbian Brigade** — 3 composite battalions; major elements 15th Bihać Inf, 17th Ključ Light; 1st Bijeljina Light "Panthers" (East Bosnian Corps); Podrinje Special Forces "Drina Wolves" (Drina Corps); SVK 7th North Dalmatian Corps battle group.
- **Tactical Group 4** (later formed) — 11th Krupa Light, 1st Novigrad Inf.

SVK contribution: 103rd Donji Lapac Light (15th Lika Corps) attacking SW of Bihać on 8 November to fix HVO/ARBiH lines.

**Phases of Štit-94:**

| Phase | Dates | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 (4 Nov) | 4 Nov | TG-3 along Bihać–Petrovac road; Balac KIA leading 1st Serbian Brigade |
| Days 2–5 (5–9 Nov) | 5–9 Nov | TG-3 takes Čukovi (~14 km N of Kulen Vakuf), Dubovsko, reaches Račić (~10 km SE of Bihać city); TG-2 takes Tihotina/Hrgar; TG-1 retakes Veliki/Mali Radić ridges and Drenovo Tijesno junction; SVK Banija forces around Krupa attack 505th/511th remnants south of Una; SVK air strike on Bihać ammo depot (Orao with Maverick from Udbina) |
| Days 6–12 (10–16 Nov) | 10–16 Nov | Brief pause for surrender ultimatum (rejected); TG-1 reaches Grmuša on Una; TG-2/TG-3 retake Ripač and Lohovska Brdo |
| Days 13–16 (17–20 Nov) | 17–20 Nov | Final ridges of Grabež recovered; Milovanović proclaims "phase one complete" |

**Outcome of phase one of Štit-94:** Most of the ARBiH October gains were recovered by 20 November. The 5th Corps was driven back to roughly its original lines on the Grabež southern half. The northern Grabež and the Radić ridges had changed hands and stayed with VRS. Total recovery for VRS: ~150–200 km² of the ~250 km² lost.

### 1.4 Phase two — Operational Group *Pauk* (Spider), November 1994 – August 1995

This is the bizarre command structure that pinned the 5th Corps for the next nine months. *Pauk* (Spider) was a joint command outside both the VRS Main Staff and the SVK Main Staff — it reported instead "in part to the General Staff of the Armed Forces of Yugoslavia and in part to the competent element of Serbian State Security" (Maj. Gen. Milisav Sekulić, *Knin Je Pao u Beogradu*, 2001, pp. 91–92, quoted at BB2 p.560 fn 29).

**Command:** Gen. **Mile Novaković** (ex-JNA, former SVK Main Staff chief, former VRS TG-2 commander during *Corridor 92*).

**Subordinate forces (BB2 p.560):**
- Two companies from 63rd Airborne Brigade / VJ Corps of Special Units (Belgrade).
- Elements of "Red Berets" Special Operations Unit / Serbian State Security (RDB).
- Elements of Arkan's Serbian Volunteer Guard (SDG).
- HQ National Defense of the APWB (Abdić).
- 1st Velika Kladuša, 2nd Cazin, 3rd Vrnograč Brigades (APWB).
- TG-8/21st Kordun Corps SVK (1 bn each from 11th Vojnić, 13th Slunj, 19th Vrginmost; 1 armd bn; 1 mixed arty bn).
- TG-9/39th Banija Corps SVK (1 bn each from 24th Glina, 31st Petrinja; 1 tank coy; 1 mixed arty bn).
- Plus TG-5, TG-6, TG-7 from 15th Lika Corps SVK (103rd Donji Lapac, 9th Gračac Mot, 18th Korenica, 50th Vrhovine, 7th North Dalmatian, 70th Plaški, "Scorpion" Special Battalion, Captain Dragan's "Alpha" Training Center).

**Total committed against the pocket Nov 1994 – Aug 1995:** Sekulić's claim of 6,638 SVK soldiers in December 1994 alone (BB2 p.560 fn 30), plus VJ/RDB/SDG/APWB ~3,000, plus VRS 2KK ~12,000 — call it 20,000+ engaged at peak. The November–December 1994 phase was opened with the SA-2 surface-to-air missiles fired in ground-to-ground mode from the 155th Air Defense Rocket Brigade.

**APWB resurrection.** Pauk's primary tactical purpose, beyond destroying the 5th Corps, was to reinstall **Fikret Abdić** in Velika Kladuša. Abdić had fled in August 1994; the *Pauk* offensive in late November captured Velika Kladuša and on 18 November APWB forces claimed 365 5th Corps prisoners (BB2 p.560 fn 35), of whom 100 reportedly "joined APWB units" (under duress, per BB2 footnote phrasing).

**Outcome of phase two (November 1994 – August 1995):**
- Bihać city not taken. The 5th Corps held the southern half of the Grabež plateau and the city itself.
- Velika Kladuša and the Cazinska Krajina north-west returned to APWB control through winter 1994–95.
- The pocket compressed but did not crack. NATO air strikes on Udbina (21 Nov 1994) cratered the runway but left aircraft on the field — deliberately limited (BB2 p.555). UN hostage-taking immediately followed.
- **Croatian intervention threat (1 Dec 1994):** Tuđman publicly warned Croatia would intervene if Bihać fell (BB2 p.560 fn 40 — Giles Elgood, Reuters). This signal was load-bearing: Belgrade and Pale knew the SVK could not handle a serious HV intervention, so the *Pauk*/Štit-94 offensive was constrained by escalation risk.

### 1.5 ARBiH 5th Corps state entering 1995

The 5th Corps after Štit-94 was:
- **Battle-hardened but exhausted.** Roughly 18,000–20,000 effectives, BB2 p.541 attributes the pre-Grmeč count at ~15,000, so the corps lost or absorbed some casualties offset by mobilisation gains.
- **Equipped slightly better** — some captured T-55s, some captured artillery, the captured Grabež barracks contents (small arms, mortars, ammunition).
- **Logistically isolated.** Resupply by air-drop and the trickle through the Bos. Krupa salient. Ammunition rationed at ~20 rounds/rifle/month per BB2's general pocket-supply remarks.
- **Internally split.** APWB held the NW pocket through winter 1994–95. The fight resumed in spring 1995 when 5th Corps began chipping at Velika Kladuša again.

**The strategic posture at the start of 1995:** "Hold the line, wait for external change." Dudaković's instinct after the Grmeč defeat was that the pocket could not break out alone. He needed either NATO air against VRS heavy weapons, or an HV intervention against the SVK, or both. He got both — but only in August.

## 2. ARBiH 7th Corps — "Just Out of Reach": Donji Vakuf 1994 (BB2 Annex 50, pp. 465–469)

The 7th Corps was established under **Brig. Gen. Mehmed Alagić** with HQ Travnik in March 1994 as one of the post-Washington reorganisation outputs (splitting 3rd Corps' Travnik-area brigades into a new corps). Its mission: drive west toward Donji Vakuf, Jajce, and ultimately the Krajina the Muslims had been ethnically cleansed from in 1992. The corps was deliberately stocked with displaced-population brigades: the 770th "Donji Vakuf" Mtn (formerly 370th/3rd C), the 707th "Bugojno" Mtn (formerly 307th/3rd C), the 727th "Banja Luka" Mtn (formerly 327th/3rd C), the 705th "Jajce" Mtn (formerly 305th/3rd C), plus the elite 17th Krajina Mtn Brigade — the corps' permanent spearhead.

### 2.1 Force composition (1994)

**ARBiH 7th Corps (Alagić)** — total ~12,000 committed at peak across Donji Vakuf and Vlašić axes (BB2 p.467 implied; per Cincar essay p.467).

| Brigade | HQ | Role |
|---|---|---|
| 17th Krajina Mountain | Travnik | spearhead — used on both Donji Vakuf and Vlašić |
| 707th "Bugojno" Mountain | Bugojno | local force, Donji Vakuf S axis |
| 770th "Donji Vakuf" Mountain | Prusac | displaced — Donji Vakuf SE axis |
| 727th "Banja Luka" Mountain | Travnik | Vlašić axis (drawn from displaced BL population) |
| 705th "Jajce" Mountain | (Travnik area) | Vlašić axis |
| 706th Mountain | Han Bila | Vlašić axis |

Plus reinforcements from 3rd Corps on Vlašić (see §3): 7th Muslim, 27th Banja Luka, 325th Vitez, 333rd Mountain (Kaćuni), elements 27th Mtn.

**Opposing VRS forces (BB2 p.469):**
- **30th Light Infantry Division (1st Krajina Corps),** Col. Jovo Blažanović, HQ Mt. Vlašić.
  - 19th Krajina Light Infantry, HQ Donji Vakuf (Srbobran) — defended town directly.
  - 11th Mrkonjić Grad Light Infantry — N of Donji Vakuf in Komar Mountains.
  - 1st Šipovo Light Infantry — W of Turbe in Komar Mountains.
- Plus ~1,500 reinforcements arriving through the year: one battalion from 1st Novigrad Infantry, composite units from 1KK; elements of 1st Military Police Battalion as "intervention unit"; 1st Mixed Artillery Regiment + 1st Mixed Antitank Artillery Brigade + at least one tank company. Total Blažanović command: ~6,000 (BB2 p.466) plus reinforcements → ~7,500 by Nov.

### 2.2 Phases of the 1994 Donji Vakuf campaign (BB2 pp.466–467)

| Phase | Dates | Action | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-spring lull | Jan – mid-Mar 1994 | Mutual shelling only | Ceasefire built up forces |
| First attack | 16–22 Mar | 7th Corps takes Mala Suljaga peak; cuts Bugojno→Donji Vakuf water supply; captures T-55 and equipment | Stopped short of town; VRS 30th Div counter-attacks 22 Mar, blunts advance but no recovery |
| Second push | 2–6 Apr | Captures Urija area; advances from Prusac (S) and Bugojno (E) to ~5 km SE of Donji Vakuf | Stopped 6 Apr |
| Build-up | Apr–May | Sporadic fighting | None decisive |
| Third push | 29 May – 11 Jun | Attacks 30th Div all along 3-bde front, Komar Mtns S of Turbe → Koščani plateau S of Donji Vakuf; takes villages 10 km SW of Turbe from 1st Šipovo and 11th Mrkonjić; tries to seize Koščani (7 km SW Donji Vakuf) on 5 Jun | Cease-fire 10 Jun ignored, fighting through 11 Jun |
| Mid-summer | mid-Jun – end-Jul | Gradual gains on Donji Vakuf and Vlašić fronts | Claimed 100 km² total in Donji Vakuf-Vlašić area by end-Jul (BB2 p.467) |
| Fourth push | 19 Oct – 11 Nov | General offensive merges with Kupres axis; assault toward Donji Vakuf merges with HVO Cincar; ARBiH pushes front line NW; 4 Nov attacks Koščani/Kopčići/Urije; 7 Nov main assault, HVO artillery support, blocked at Prusac (7 km S) | Donji Vakuf NOT taken; HVO took Kupres 3 Nov; 7th Corps redirected effort N |
| Final push | 16–20 Nov | Shelling and infantry on Koščani | Inconclusive |
| Winter freeze | end-Nov – Dec | Bitter inconclusive fighting along ridge lines | Trench warfare, both sides dig in |

### 2.3 Strategic significance

**Donji Vakuf was not taken in 1994.** Five major pushes, eight months of fighting, ~100 km² gained in the broader Donji Vakuf–Vlašić salient — but the town itself remained VRS-held under the renamed identity "Srbobran." It would not fall until **13 September 1995** during *Maestral 2*, when the HV/HVO drive from the south finally collapsed the 30th Division's western shoulder.

**Lessons that mattered for 1995:**
1. **Light infantry cannot dislodge VRS dug-in defenders with artillery support.** BB2 p.466 is explicit: "Even the expert infiltrators of the ARBiH infantry would have to close with and slug it out with VRS units that were dug in and backed by armor and artillery. It was to be a slow, grinding series of attacks to take bits of ground from the VRS."
2. **Combined operations work; sequential operations don't.** The November 1994 assaults coordinated with the HVO Kupres push were the only ones that produced strategic-level VRS displacement (the 7th Kupres-Šipovo Mot Bde fell back from Kupres to Šipovo, which is why Šipovo was the soft point in *Maestral 2*).
3. **The 7th Corps now had a year of bad terrain experience.** Its spring–autumn 1995 build-up under the new Corps-and-Division structure was informed by what Vlašić and Donji Vakuf had taught it.

## 3. Back and Forth on Mt. Vlašić, 1994 (BB2 Annex 55, pp. 491–493)

This is the user's flagged operation. There is **no single named "Operation Tvigrad"** in BB2's account — the 1994 Vlašić campaign was a continuous series of pushes by the 7th Corps (reinforced with 3rd Corps elements) rather than a discrete operation with a code-name. "Tvigrad" appears occasionally in informal/secondary sources as a popular Bosnian name for one of the spring 1994 phases, but BB2, ICTY filings, and Hoare do not use it. The campaign as documented:

### 3.1 Strategic significance of the Vlašić plateau

The Vlašić feature (highest peak Mt. Paljenik, 1,933 m) overlooked the entire western Lašva valley. From its slopes the VRS could shell Travnik and Zenica at will. It hosted a key military communications facility and the civilian TV/radio transmitters serving central Bosnia. **"The gateway to Donji Vakuf and Jajce — and that gate was firmly closed"** (BB2 p.491).

Possession history: seized by HVO at the start of war; JNA occupied the summit May 1992; VRS retained it thereafter. Travnik fell to ARBiH 3rd Corps in June 1993 (after the Croat-Bosniak war began); Vlašić remained the immediate VRS threat to Travnik.

### 3.2 Force composition (1994) — BB2 p.493

**ARBiH 7th Corps + 3rd Corps elements (Alagić):**
- 7th Muslim Brigade, HQ Zenica (3rd Corps subordinate, OPCON to 7th Corps for Vlašić ops) — most or all during attacks.
- 17th Krajina Mountain Brigade, HQ Travnik — at least one battalion normally, possibly entire brigade during attacks.
- 27th Banja Luka Mountain Brigade, HQ Travnik — most or all during attacks.
- 305th Jajce Mountain Brigade — one battalion normally, entire brigade during attacks.
- 306th Mountain Brigade, HQ Han Bila — entire brigade during attacks.
- 325th Vitez Mountain Brigade, HQ S of Vitez — at least one battalion.
- 333rd Mountain Brigade, HQ Kaćuni — possibly one battalion.

**Opposing VRS — "Vlašić" Operational Group / 1st Krajina Corps (BB2 p.491, p.493):**
- HQ Vitovlje, Lt. Col. **Janko Trivić** (later VRS Drina Corps commander).
- 1st Kotor Varoš Light Infantry, AOR Javorak-Meokrnje NE of Vlašić.
- 22nd Infantry Brigade, HQ Skender Vakuf (Knežvo) — deployed on Vlašić itself, opposite Turbe.
- 1st Knežvo Light Infantry, HQ Skender Vakuf — behind 22nd near Babanovac.
- Supported by artillery battalion, one tank company. Defended from concrete bunkers behind **four concentric rings of minefields** (BB2 p.492).

### 3.3 Phases of the 1994 Vlašić campaign

| Phase | Dates | Action | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| First assault | late Mar – Apr 1994 | Secondary effort to Donji Vakuf attack; breakthrough at 1st Kotor Varoš / 22nd Inf Bde boundary in Meokrnje-Javorak area on 28 Apr; some tactical gains | VRS contains 29 Apr; recaptures ground over next 2 weeks |
| Major encirclement attempt | 24 May – mid-June | Attack along entire Vlašić frontline trying to encircle the mountain | "Close-run thing" — VRS reserves all but exhausted; ARBiH cannot complete encirclement; VRS holds line and resupply |
| VRS counterattack | ~25 July | VRS hits Javorak area on SE Vlašić | Inconclusive, little ground changes hands |
| Last major push | early Sept 1994 | ARBiH attempts to take summit | Gains some ridge lines, falls short of goal; VRS counter-attack 6 Sept in Vlašić-Turbe area, inconclusive |
| Winter freeze | Oct-Nov 1994 | ARBiH redirects forces to Donji Vakuf and Kupres axes | VRS continues artillery shelling of Travnik and Zenica unhindered |

### 3.4 Strategic outcome

**The summit was never taken in 1994.** BB2 p.492 is the canonical assessment:
> "The Bosnian Army's 1994 campaigns had tied down some VRS forces during ARBiH attacks elsewhere and won some modest territorial gains, but had done little to force the offending artillery off the Vlašić plateau. The Serbs could still shell Travnik and Zenica from the heights with impunity, and they made the ARBiH pay for its offensives by stepping up their shelling of the two towns. Weighed against the high casualties the ARBiH 7th Corps had sustained in its offensives, the Bosnian Army leadership had to accept that costs had probably exceeded gains."

**Vlašić's eventual fall came in September 1995** as part of the same Maestral 2 / 7th Corps Donji Vakuf-axis offensive — the Vlašić TV repeater fell to 7th Corps on 13–20 September 1995 (per `20260523_RESEARCH_ARBIH_FALL_1995.md` summary table). What had been impossible for the lightly-armed 1994 ARBiH became possible only when (a) NATO degraded VRS heavy weapons coordination via *Deliberate Force*; (b) the HV/HVO drove into the 30th Division from the south at Šipovo/Donji Vakuf, forcing Trivić's force to choose between holding Vlašić and holding its rear; and (c) ARBiH had reorganised into divisions with more combat power than the 1994 brigades.

### 3.5 "Operation Tvigrad" naming question

The user mentioned "Operation Tvigrad." Searching BB1, BB2, ICTY filings, and the Wikipedia operations article catalogue, the codename **does not appear** in canonical military-historical sources. It may be:
- A Bosnian-language popular name for one of the spring 1994 phases (most likely the May–June encirclement attempt) — Tvigrad ("Strong-City") would fit the ARBiH naming pattern of fortress/honour names (cf. Tvrtko).
- A scenario-game artefact from secondary sources.
- A misattribution — possibly conflating with HVO Operation Maestral or with the Croatian/ARBiH planning name for one of the late 1994 attacks.

**Recommendation for canon:** treat the 1994 Vlašić campaign as a continuous, multi-phase 7th Corps operation set rather than a single named operation, unless a primary source (ICTY filing, ARBiH General Staff document) attests the "Tvigrad" name. The September 1995 capture is the canonical "Vlašić" historical event for engine purposes.

## 4. Tešanj-Teslić Offensive (BB2 Annex 53, pp. 478–482)

After ten months of defending against the *Tesanj-94* VRS attacks (Dec 1993 – Mar 1994), the 3rd Corps-administered **Operations Group 7-South** (under 2nd Corps until mid/late 1994, then transferred to 3rd Corps) switched to the offensive immediately after the 19 March 1994 local HVO-ARBiH ceasefire (which preceded the formal Washington Agreement by two days but operationally enabled the redirection).

### 4.1 Force composition (BB2 p.479)

**ARBiH OG 7-South:** ~10,000 troops in four brigades + one independent battalion. Spearhead: the displaced **204th "Teslić" Mountain Brigade** (composed of Muslims ethnically cleansed from Teslić in 1992).

**HVO 110th "Usora" Brigade:** ~2,000 troops, NE corner of enclave — remained operationally allied to ARBiH throughout the Croat-Bosniak war.

**HVO 111th "Žepče" Brigade:** opposing force during the Croat-Bosniak war; supplied ~2 tanks to the May 1994 ARBiH assault after Washington Agreement.

**VRS opposing — 1st Krajina Corps "Doboj" OG 9 / Tactical Group 2 (TG-2):**
- 16th Krajina Motorized (TG-2 HQ, strongest formation).
- 1st Teslić Infantry — defending Teslić directly.
- 1st Gradiška Light Infantry.
- 1st Krnjin Light Infantry (probable).
- 2nd Teslić Light Infantry (opposite Žepče HVO enclave, further S).
- 27th Motorized — westernmost OG 9 unit.
- Plus 1st MP Bn, 1st Reconnaissance-Sabotage Co, 2nd Armored Brigade elements at corps level. At least one OG/corps-level artillery battalion.

### 4.2 Phases

| Phase | Dates | Action | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| First offensive | 23 Mar – early Apr | 204th "Teslić" Mtn Bde drives west; 1–2 km gains along 6 km front against 1st Teslić Inf Bde; high ground south of Tešanj and Teslić taken; assault on critical 577 m Husar elevation | Halted 5–6 km E of Teslić |
| Build-up | Apr–May | Sporadic fighting | Forces rebuilt |
| Second offensive | 22 May | Limited objective: capture a few more pieces of high ground; HVO 111th Žepče provides 2 tanks | Limited gains; line of peaks 5–15 km SE of Teslić gained from 1st Krajina Corps brigades |
| Summer-autumn fighting | Jun – Sep | Continued probing | No major change |

### 4.3 Strategic outcome

**Teslić was not taken.** The Tešanj enclave remained an isolated salient, the front line was pushed 1–2 km west on the southern shoulder, but Teslić itself remained VRS-held. The operation is best understood as **defensive consolidation** — turning a 10,000-troop ARBiH pocket from a passive defender into an active threat that pinned 1KK forces (1st MP Bn, 27th Motorized, 16th Krajina Motorized) that could otherwise have reinforced the Posavina corridor or Donji Vakuf.

The OG 7-South command transfer from 2nd Corps to 3rd Corps in mid/late 1994 is a structural signal: the 3rd Corps was being expanded as the new framework for central Bosnia operations, foreshadowing the January 1995 Corps-and-Division reorganisation.

When *Pauk* reinstalled APWB in late November, that freedom of action ended for nine months.

## 6. ARBiH 1st Corps and Sarajevo Theatre, Summer 1994 – Summer 1995

The Sarajevo theatre is the second-largest force concentration in Bosnia (1st Corps ~50,000–55,000 vs SRK ~30,000–35,000 by 1995), but BB2's annex coverage of the 1994 actions in Sarajevo is thinner than for the Krajina campaigns. Key markers from the broader BB and from `EVENTS_1994_1995_RESEARCH.md`:

### 6.1 Sarajevo exclusion zone (February–April 1994)

- 5 Feb 1994: Markale market massacre, 68 killed.
- 9 Feb 1994: NATO ultimatum — VRS heavy weapons must be withdrawn or placed under UN control within 20 km exclusion zone around Sarajevo.
- 17–20 Feb 1994: Heavy weapons withdrawal more or less complied with (with workarounds — some weapons hidden, some kept at UN-monitored sites with arrangements to retrieve).
- Effect on 1st Corps: tactical relief from artillery during 1994; but the SRK siege ring of light infantry and snipers remained.

### 6.2 Goražde crisis (April 1994 — VRS *Zvezda-94*)

- VRS Herzegovina Corps offensive against Goražde enclave (held by ARBiH 81st Goražde Division / East Bosnian OG).
- NATO airstrikes 10–11 April (limited, two SRK targets — VRS retaliation: UN hostages).
- 22 April: Second NATO ultimatum (20 km Goražde exclusion zone).
- 24 April: VRS pulls back.
- Casualties: ~300 ARBiH KIA / ~1,000 WIA — 16% casualty rate in the defenders (BB2 p.461).
- Effect on broader theatre: 1st Corps could not redeploy to support Goražde. Demonstrated the safe-area doctrine's vulnerability — preview of Srebrenica.

### 6.3 1st Corps 1994 — limited offensive activity

Per BB1/BB2 and the broader chronology, the 1st Corps in 1994 conducted:
- **Žuč Hill area** — small-scale local attacks NW of Sarajevo to push SRK lines back from the city.
- **Mt. Igman** — defensive consolidation around the Igman corridor that supplied the city via the Tunnel.
- **No major breakout attempts.** The 1st Corps under Vahid Karavelić in 1994 was constrained: heavy weapons removed (theirs as well as VRS's under the exclusion zone), the city's population blockade-fed, ammunition rationed.

### 6.4 1st + 4th Corps spring–summer 1995 — Treskavica / Trnovo

This is the principal pre-Sana 95 1st Corps operation, and the only ARBiH 1995 first-half operation of corps-scale outside Bihać:

- **June 1995:** 1st Corps + 4th Corps (Konjic, ~12,000–15,000) launch from Igman/Bjelašnica to take Trnovo and the Treskavica massif south of Sarajevo. ICTY filings (Karadžić IT-95-5/18-T) reference this as part of the broader breakout-attempt complex.
- **Forces:** 1st Corps Trnovo brigades + 4th/6th Corps Konjic elements + some 7th Corps loaned units. Total ~25,000 across multiple sectors.
- **Outcome:** Treskavica massif captured June–July 1995; Trnovo taken late June (briefly lost to VRS *Spreča-95* counter-offensive in late July). Bjelašnica foothills shifted to ARBiH but Sarajevo siege did not lift.
- **Strategic significance:** Forced VRS Sarajevo-Romanija Corps and the Herzegovina Corps to commit reserves SOUTH of Sarajevo, which (a) prevented those reserves from reinforcing Srebrenica during the July 1995 Drina Corps operation, and (b) tied them down for the August NATO/RRF strike phase. The Treskavica campaign was strategically a successful pinning operation even if it did not break the siege.

### 6.5 Force allocation implications

A 1st Corps that was 50,000+ on paper but operationally tied to defending Sarajevo could not contribute to the western Krajina campaign in any major way. The ARBiH that fought *Sana 95* was 5th Corps + 7th Corps + select 3rd Corps elements — not the 1st Corps. The 1st Corps' contribution to the autumn 1995 cascade was the Treskavica operation, which pinned VRS reserves, plus the political-survival role of holding the capital.

## 7. ARBiH 3rd Corps + 7th Corps vs HVO 1993–94 (Croat-Bosniak War, summarised)

The Croat-Bosniak war (`croat_bosniak_war_begins_1993` essay; ICTY IT-04-74-T Prlić Trial Judgment) ran roughly **April 1993 – 18 March 1994**. ARBiH gains in central Bosnia by spring 1994:

| Town | Date taken | Force |
|---|---|---|

| Bugojno | July 1993 | 3rd Corps (later 7th C) |
| Fojnica | July 1993 | 3rd Corps |
| Konjic surrounds | mid-1993 | 4th Corps |
| Gornji Vakuf (control) | Jan–Mar 1994 (contested) | 3rd Corps |
| Vareš | Nov 1993 (HVO+ARBiH cooperative against VRS, then HVO fled) | 3rd Corps |
| Vitez-Busovača enclave | Besieged but never broken | 3rd Corps |

| Prozor / Rama | HVO retained | — |

**Strategic state at Washington Agreement (18 Mar 1994):** ARBiH held the central Bosnia interior (Zenica, Travnik, Tuzla, the Bihać pocket, the besieged eastern enclaves, the Mostar east bank). HVO held the western Herzegovina rectangle, north Bosnia along the Sava (with VRS-held intermissions), and the Vitez/Busovača/Kiseljak enclaves in the middle of ARBiH territory. The Washington Agreement froze this line and required both sides to redeploy from facing each other to facing the VRS.

**Force release for ARBiH after Washington Agreement:**
- 3rd Corps frees 7th Muslim, 17th Krajina, 305th Jajce, 306th, 307th Bugojno (becomes 707th), 325th Vitez, 327th Banja Luka (becomes 727th), 333rd Mountain — total ~15,000 troops for offensive operations against VRS.
- 4th Corps frees Mostar-East defenders → ~8,000–12,000 troops for HV/HVO-coordinated southern operations.
- 6th Corps (Konjic) frees ~10,000 for combined ops with 4th C in Herzegovina sector.

**This was the manpower foundation for the 1994 attacks on Donji Vakuf, Vlašić, Tešanj-Teslić, and Kupres.**

## 8. HVO Operation *Cincar* (November 1994)

This is the first significant federation-coordinated joint operation. Treated in detail in `data/scenarios/essays/operation_cincar_1994.json` (citing IT-04-74-T and IT-95-5/18-T).

### 8.1 Force composition (per the Cincar essay, BB2 reference)

- **ARBiH 7th Corps (Alagić):** ~12,000 troops. Bore the initial breakthrough phase from 20 October 1994.
- **HVO Tomislavgrad Operational Zone:** ~5,000 troops. Entered 1 November 1994 ("Cincar" phase proper).
- Plus HV artillery and logistical support (unspecified scale, less documented than for 1995 operations).

### 8.2 Phases

| Phase | Dates | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 (ARBiH) | 20–31 Oct 1994 | 7th Corps from Bugojno against Kupres Gate (Kupreška vrata); slow gains in mountain terrain |
| Phase 2 (HVO Cincar) | 1–3 Nov 1994 | HVO from Livno/Prozor; Kupres town captured 3 Nov |
| Phase 3 (consolidation) | 4–10 Nov 1994 | ARBiH 7th Corps redirected N toward Donji Vakuf; ~600 km² total liberated |

### 8.3 Strategic significance — the precondition for Mistral 2

**Kupres was the precondition for *Mistral 2* in September 1995.** With Kupres lost, the VRS 7th Kupres-Šipovo Motorized Brigade fell back to Šipovo. Šipovo was therefore the soft point in the 1KK / 2KK boundary that HV 4th and 7th Guards Brigades broke through on 8 September 1995. Without Cincar, *Mistral 2* would have had to take Kupres before Šipovo, lengthening the campaign and exposing Croatia to NATO's eventual ceasefire pressure.

**Lessons demonstrated:**
1. **ARBiH bears the breakthrough phase, HVO bears the exploitation.** This pattern recurs in *Maestral 2* (where the HV 4th & 7th Guards Brigades exploited and the 7th Corps held the shoulder) and *Sana 95* (where 5th Corps held and HV pressed from the north).
2. **The Federation alliance can execute coordinated operations in the field**, not just on paper.
3. **Belgrade embargo on RS (imposed Aug 1994 — `belgrade_embargo_rs_1994.json`) was beginning to constrain VRS logistics by November**, eroding the heavy-weapons advantage that had been the VRS's primary edge.

### 8.4 Cost

Casualties for Cincar are not well-documented in BB2. ICTY filings reference the 600 km² captured (Cincar essay). Hoare cites ARBiH 7th Corps casualties for the entire October–November 1994 campaign (Donji Vakuf + Vlašić + Kupres) as "well over 1,000 KIA"; HVO casualties for Cincar proper ~150–200 KIA (estimates).

## 9. VRS Counter-Operations Early 1995

The user asked about "VRS Operation Vrbas-95 / Vlašić counteroffensive (early 1995)." **There is no VRS operation named *Vrbas-95* in BB2 or ICTY filings.** The closest equivalents:

### 9.1 VRS *Spreča-95* (Spring 1995, central-east)

VRS limited counter-offensive in the Spreča valley (Tuzla axis) and around Olovo, designed to push back ARBiH 2nd Corps gains from late 1994. Limited success — VRS recovered some hills, did not retake major settlements. Coverage in BB2 is brief.

### 9.2 VRS late June – early July 1995 — the Drina enclaves

- **Operation *Krivaja-95*** — Drina Corps offensive against Srebrenica (6–11 July 1995, fall of enclave on 11 July; massacres follow 13–22 July). Karadžić, Mladić, Krstić, Beara — ICTY core convictions.
- **Operation *Stupčanica-95*** — Drina Corps offensive against Žepa (14–25 July 1995, fall of enclave).
- These are not "counteroffensives" in the sense the user asked — they were the VRS's planned reduction of the eastern Muslim enclaves, exploiting the constrained safe-area doctrine.

### 9.3 VRS / 1KK actions on Vlašić, spring 1995

The VRS 1KK 30th Division and Vlašić OG launched limited counter-attacks in spring 1995 — recovering some 1994 ARBiH ridge gains in the Komar Mountains. Not a major named operation; rather, defensive consolidation by the 30th Light Infantry Division ahead of expected ARBiH spring offensive (which arrived in summer through the new corps-and-division structure).

### 9.4 The strategic VRS posture entering 1995

VRS Main Staff (Mladić) priorities in order:
1. **Maintain the Posavina corridor** (Brčko-Modriča-Doboj) at all costs — the only land link between RS and Serbia/SVK.
2. **Reduce the eastern enclaves** (Srebrenica, Žepa) — political-strategic goal for "ethnically homogenous east-Bosnia RS."
3. **Hold the Sarajevo siege** for political leverage.
4. **Pin the 5th Corps** via *Pauk* and APWB.
5. **Defensive consolidation everywhere else.**

The VRS in 1995 was no longer the army that had taken 70% of Bosnia in 1992. It was an army stretched along too long a front, with declining manpower (Belgrade embargo cutting recruits, FRY VJ no longer covertly rotating troops in), low morale outside the elite units (Drina Wolves, 1st MP Battalions, 65th Protection Motorized Regiment), and a Belgrade benefactor (Milošević) increasingly interested in distancing himself in exchange for sanctions relief.

## 10. ARBiH 2nd Corps and the Posavina Corridor 1994

The user asked whether there was a major 1994 ARBiH offensive against the Posavina corridor (Brčko-Modriča-Doboj line). **There was not a major offensive in 1994.** What happened in the 2nd Corps area in 1994:

- **Pinning operations along Sapna, Teočak, Gradačac front.** Continuous low-level pressure on the VRS East Bosnian Corps (Gen. Novica Simić) and the southern edge of 1st Krajina Corps (around Brčko).
- **Maglaj enclave relief.** The Maglaj pocket (held by ARBiH 3rd Corps elements, partially supplied through HVO 110th "Usora" Brigade in Tešanj-Maglaj area) was the focus of defensive operations through 1994 — the VRS *Tesanj-94* offensive Dec 1993 – Mar 1994 was contained by ARBiH OG 7-South.
- **No corps-scale offensive against the corridor itself.** The Posavina corridor narrowed to ~4 km at Brčko (the Arizona market chokepoint) and the VRS heavy weapons concentration there — the 1st Bijeljina Light "Panthers," parts of 1st Krajina Corps armor — made it the most heavily defended VRS sector outside Sarajevo. The 2nd Corps lacked the artillery to break it.

**1994 2nd Corps operations were therefore defensive/pinning** — denying VRS Main Staff the ability to redeploy East Bosnian Corps forces to other fronts. The Panthers (1st Bijeljina) were drawn into *Breza-94* and *Štit-94* against the 5th Corps precisely because the 2nd Corps could not threaten Brčko decisively enough to keep them home.

The 2nd Corps would not break out toward the corridor until autumn 1995, and even then only marginally — the corridor remained VRS-held through Dayton (and post-Dayton was assigned to RS as the Brčko District after arbitration).

## 11. ARBiH State Entering 1995 — The Cross-Cutting Picture

Per the user's framing question: **What was the ARBiH state entering 1995 (after January reorganization but before Storm)?**

### 11.1 Manpower

- **Total strength:** ~180,000–200,000 (`ARBIH_ORDER_OF_BATTLE_MASTER.md` p.53; growing toward 200,000–230,000 by mid-year as mobilisation intensified post-Washington and corps-and-division reorganization).
- **Distribution roughly:**
  - 1st Corps Sarajevo: ~50,000–55,000.
  - 2nd Corps Tuzla: ~45,000–50,000.
  - 3rd Corps Zenica: ~50,000–55,000.
  - 4th Corps Mostar + 6th Corps Konjic: ~20,000–25,000 combined.
  - 5th Corps Bihać: ~20,000–25,000 (after APWB temporarily contained).
  - 7th Corps Travnik: ~15,000–18,000.
  - Plus enclaves (Srebrenica, Žepa, Goražde): ~12,000–15,000.

### 11.2 Equipment

- **Small arms:** Adequate by 1995 — production at Vitez, Travnik, Goražde munitions plants now back online under ARBiH control; smuggling through Croatia open after Washington Agreement; some US covert support post-Croat-Bosniak war.
- **Mortars:** 60mm, 82mm, 120mm — adequate quantities, ammunition rationing.
- **Artillery:** Still a chronic weakness. Captured 122mm D-30, 130mm M-46, occasional 155mm M114; no organic artillery production except mortar tubes. Hoare estimates ~200 artillery tubes total across the entire ARBiH, vs ~800+ in the VRS.
- **Armor:** Marginal. Maybe 30–50 tanks total across all corps, mostly captured T-55s, a few M-84s. VRS had ~300–400 tanks.
- **Air defense:** SA-7 Strela MANPADS (Stinger-class shoulder-launched), some SA-9 Strela-1, captured AAA. Effective against helicopters; less against fixed-wing.

### 11.3 Training and command coherence

- **The January 1995 Corps-and-Division reorganization** restructured the corps from Operational Groups (1992 TO-style geographic units) into divisions with internal brigades. The 5th Corps got the model up first — its OB by September 1995 was a divisional structure (501st-517th brigades grouped). The 7th Corps and 3rd Corps reorganization followed through spring–summer 1995.
- **Command coherence improved** with the dismissal of Sefer Halilović (1993) and the consolidation of authority under **Gen. Rasim Delić** as Chief of Main Staff, supported by Enver Hadžihasanović as later Chief of General Staff.
- **Mid-level officer corps maturing.** Field commanders like Dudaković, Alagić, Karavelić, Sead Delić, Sakib Mahmuljin had three years of war experience, knew their formations, and had institutional trust. This was qualitatively different from the JNA-defector / TO-officer mix of 1992.

### 11.4 Supply

- **Sarajevo:** Tunnel (completed 1993) supplied the city. Limited but stable.
- **Bihać:** Air-drops, the Cazin–Croatia smuggling route (intermittent), and post-August 1995 the direct Croatian land bridge.
- **Central Bosnia / Tuzla:** Open supply via Croatia after Washington Agreement; the Vitez–Travnik–Zenica corridor functional.
- **Eastern enclaves:** Critical — Srebrenica, Žepa supply minimal, dependent on UN convoys frequently blocked. Goražde better (closer to Sarajevo, occasional helicopter resupply).
- **Overall:** Better than at any prior point but still constrained. Hoare estimates ARBiH 1995 ammunition consumption ratio per soldier-month at ~25–40% of VRS, ~10% of NATO doctrinal norms.

### 11.5 Operational lessons learned 1994 → 1995

| Lesson from 1994 | 1995 application |
|---|---|
| Light infantry alone cannot break dug-in VRS positions | Larger concentrations (3+ brigades/objective), seek operations where VRS is also under pressure elsewhere |
| Sequential operations fail; combined ops with HVO/HV work | Sana 95 timed to Maestral 2 + post-Storm SVK destruction + NATO Deliberate Force |
| Surprise + concentration wins first 48 hours; logistics fails the third week | Plan around exogenous enablers (NATO C2 degradation, refugee road-paralysis) |
| Two-front war (APWB) drains 5th Corps below offensive threshold | Reduce APWB in spring–summer 1995 (Sloboda 95) before main offensive |
| Vlašić-style attritional offensives are not strategically valuable | Mass against the western frontage where VRS depth is collapsing, not the central VRS fortified zone |
| The VRS will reinforce from 1KK and Main Staff reserves | Engineer multiple simultaneous threats so Main Staff cannot concentrate (Treskavica pins; Bihać + Kupres + Posavina probes simultaneous) |

The ARBiH that broke out in September 1995 had **lost in October 1994 and learned**. That is the load-bearing characterisation. The autumn 1995 cascade was not a surprise breakthrough — it was a planned exploitation of an external collapse that the ARBiH had been preparing for since the Štit-94 defeat.

## 13. Key Findings for Engine Synthesis (no proposals)

For the team building the engine, the 1994 record establishes these load-bearing facts:

1. **The pre-1995 ARBiH cannot win an offensive against dug-in VRS without external enablers.** Every 1994 named offensive that targeted a strongly-defended town stalled short of the objective. Donji Vakuf stayed VRS-held in 1994, 1994's-end, and through spring–summer 1995. Vlašić summit stayed VRS-held. Teslić stayed VRS-held. Bos. Krupa held against ARBiH (and then fell to VRS counter-attack). The pattern is consistent: **a 1994 ARBiH brigade is not capable of dislodging a 1994 VRS brigade from prepared positions even at numerical parity, because the VRS has artillery, armor support, and bunker/minefield infrastructure.**

2. **The historical state in spring 1995 is "stalemate with rising ARBiH manpower advantage."** The ARBiH was getting larger faster than the VRS, but the offensive balance remained roughly stable because terrain and equipment advantages still favoured the defender. Engine modeling needs to reproduce: ARBiH manpower growth from ~165–180k to ~200k over 1994; VRS manpower stable around ~80k effectives; tank/artillery ratio still ~5:1 VRS favour.

3. **Two-front fights cripple a corps.** The 5th Corps could not simultaneously fight APWB and the VRS at full intensity. When APWB was suppressed (Aug 1994), 5th Corps won at Grmeč. When APWB was reinstalled (Nov 1994 via *Pauk*), 5th Corps could not break out. **An engine "interior front" mechanic (which the AWWV repo has via the HRHB-RBiH internal front during the Croat-Bosniak war) is structurally necessary for modeling the 5th Corps state through 1994–95.**

4. **The HVO-ARBiH joint operations work when ARBiH does the breakthrough and HVO does the exploitation, not vice versa.** Cincar is the canonical example. *Maestral 2* repeats the pattern in 1995 (with HV in the exploitation role). An engine modeling federation joint operations needs to encode this division-of-labour, not treat HVO and ARBiH as fungible federation forces.

5. **VRS Main Staff has reserves it will commit to crises.** Milovanović personally took command at Štit-94, drew composite battalions from every corps (except Herzegovina), borrowed armor from 1KK, and pulled in SVK forces. **The reserve-pooling mechanism is essential to the VRS 1993–94 defensive posture.** It then *fails* in autumn 1995 because Main Staff reserves are exhausted (Srebrenica/Žepa absorbed them in July) and the SVK is destroyed. The 1995 collapse is not because reserves were not committed — it is because there were no reserves left.

6. **The Vlašić plateau is a fortress, not a battleground.** Four concentric rings of minefields, concrete bunkers, three brigades + artillery battalion + tank company defending a 1,933 m summit. Engine modeling of mountain fortresses needs to apply a much stronger defensive multiplier than generic urban or rural terrain. Vlašić-class objectives should be effectively immune to brigade-level assault and require either (a) outflanking and supply-cutting (which is what eventually worked in Sep 1995) or (b) heavy artillery suppression (which the ARBiH never had).

7. **Operation names are not always operations.** "Operation Tvigrad" appears in scenario-game and popular literature but is not corroborated by primary military-historical sources (BB1/BB2, ICTY filings, Hoare). The 1994 Vlašić campaign is a multi-phase, multi-month set of attacks, not a discrete codename. Care is needed in choosing engine operation names that are historically attested.

8. **The Bihać pocket model is fundamentally different from the rest of Bosnia.** *Pauk* command outside both VRS and SVK chains, RDB and VJ involvement, an APWB puppet, Croatian intervention threat — none of these patterns occur elsewhere. The 5th Corps is fighting a different war from the other corps. Engine modeling of the pocket needs distinct mechanics (no land supply, multiple hostile factions, APZB as a separate intra-Bosniak faction with its own brigades, a "Croatian intervention threat" gating VRS operations).
