# Research: ARBiH Attempted Operations to Break the Siege of Sarajevo, 1992–1995

**Date:** 2026-05-23
**Author:** historian (research dispatch)
**Purpose:** Historical reference for the AWWV deterministic simulation. Documents every major ARBiH offensive attempt to lift, breach, or loosen the Sarajevo siege from April 1992 through the September 1995 ceasefire. The siege was never broken by ARBiH ground action — this report explains *why* through force composition, terrain, and the firepower asymmetry that pinned the 1st Corps for 1,425 days.
**Scope:** ARBiH 1st Corps (Sarajevo, inside the ring) and 4th/6th Corps (Konjic/Igman, outside the ring) offensive attempts against SRK; siege-loosening events of August–September 1995; the Igman tunnel as logistic substitute for ground breakout.
**Source hierarchy:** ICTY Galić (IT-98-29-T trial 5 Dec 2003 / appeal 30 Nov 2006), Karadžić (IT-95-5/18-T, Vol. III paras 4920–5031), Milošević (IT-02-54) contextual; Balkan Battlegrounds Vol. 2 (CIA OREA, 2002), pp. 144–195, 386–402; Hoare, *How Bosnia Armed* (Saqi 2004), ch. 4–7; Burg & Shoup, *The War in Bosnia-Herzegovina* (M.E. Sharpe 1999); Sarajevo Tunnel Museum primary record (overriding Wikipedia on construction dates). Wikipedia used only for date-level filler.

## 0. Executive Summary

Between **2 May 1992** (first JNA artillery bombardment of Sarajevo) and **29 February 1996** (formal lifting of the siege under Dayton), the ARBiH 1st Corps inside the city and 4th/6th Corps outside it launched at least **eight identifiable offensive operations** intended to break, breach, or significantly loosen the siege. **None succeeded in restoring a contiguous ARBiH-held land corridor** between Sarajevo and Federation-held central Bosnia. What loosened the siege in late 1995 was not ARBiH ground action but **NATO Operation *Deliberate Force*** (30 Aug – 20 Sept 1995) plus **UK/French Rapid Reaction Force artillery** from Mt. Igman, both responses to the Markale II massacre (28 Aug 1995). The siege ended structurally on **20 September 1995** when the Total Exclusion Zone forced VRS heavy weapons to withdraw 20 km from the city centre.

| Operation | Dates | ARBiH forces | Axis | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Jug-92 / early breakouts** | May–July 1992 | 1st Corps (forming), TO Sarajevo | Outward from urban perimeter; Stup, Vraca, Nedžarići | **Failed.** Stalemate ring fixes by mid-July 1992. |
| **Igman tunnel construction** | 28 Jan – 30 Jul 1993 | TO + 1st Corps engineers, civilians | Under Sarajevo airport runway | **Succeeded** as logistic bypass, not breakout. 760–800 m, ~340 m/day completion rate. |
| **Operation *Lukavac-93*** | June–August 1993 | 1st + 6th Corps (Bjelašnica/Igman) | Trnovo–Bjelašnica–Igman ridge | **Failed.** VRS counter-offensive (same name, VRS *Lukavac-93*) seized Igman/Bjelašnica 4 Aug 1993; UN/UNPROFOR demilitarisation under Owen-Stoltenberg froze the line. |
| **Trebević 1993–1994** | autumn 1993 – spring 1994 | 1st Corps (101st, 102nd Motorised Bdes) | Up Trebević massif east of Sarajevo | **Limited tactical gains** on lower slopes (Debelo Brdo, Mojmilo); never took the ridge crest. |
| **Žuč / Hum / Stupsko Brdo attritional fighting** | 1992 – Sept 1995 | 1st Corps (1st, 2nd, 3rd, 5th Mountain Bdes) | Northern urban perimeter | **Limited gains 1992–93** (Žuč hill 8 Dec 1992 — sole symbolic victory); attritional thereafter. |
| **Operation *Tekbir-95*** | 15 June – early July 1995 | 1st + 4th Corps, ~25,000 men | Trnovo – Treskavica – outer ring south | **Failed.** Initial gains on Treskavica; VRS counter-attack with Drina Corps reinforcement retook Trnovo 16 July. |
| **Treskavica recapture** | mid-June 1995 → July 1995 | 4th + 6th Corps elements | Treskavica massif (south of Igman) | **Captured then lost.** Held early-mid July, lost by 19–20 July under VRS counter-pressure coincident with Srebrenica fall. |
| **September 1995 ground push** | 17 Sept – 14 Oct 1995 | 1st Corps + 4th Corps elements | Renewed pressure Treskavica, Trnovo, Žuč | **Stalemate.** Trnovo recaptured 5 Aug 1995 by ARBiH/HVO from south, but Sarajevo's immediate ring did not move; siege ended administratively, not militarily. |

The structural answer to *why couldn't ARBiH break out for 3.5 years* is in §10: **SRK held the heights, owned the heavy weapons, and was supplied from the VRS Main Staff rear; ARBiH 1st Corps attacked uphill, had no artillery to suppress, and could not pass armour or heavy logistics through the only corridor (Mt. Igman footpath / tunnel).**

## 1. Operation *Jug-92* and Early 1992 Breakout Attempts

### 1.1 Context

The siege ring closed in stages April–May 1992. JNA/VRS forces (formally split 19 May 1992 when JNA elements in BiH became VRS) seized the dominating high ground in a deliberate sequence: Vraca police academy (3 April), Nedžarići barracks complex (early April), Lukavica barracks (south), Pale (east, SRK HQ from June 1992), Ilidža (west), Vogošća (north), Ilijaš (north-east), Hadžići (south-west). By the end of May 1992 the ARBiH-controlled portion of the city was a roughly 30 km² urban pocket surrounded by hills held by JNA/VRS units that became, on **22 May 1992**, the **Sarajevo-Romanija Corps (SRK)** under Maj. Gen. Tomislav Šipčić, then Gen. Stanislav Galić from **10 September 1992**, then Gen. Dragomir Milošević from **10 August 1994**.

### 1.2 Forces

| Side | Formation | Approx. strength May–July 1992 |
|---|---|---|
| ARBiH (forming) | Territorial Defence Sarajevo Regional Staff, MUP "Bosna", "Patriotic League" cells, "Green Berets" | 30,000–45,000 lightly-armed (BB2 p. 152: ~40 tanks, mostly small arms) |
| VRS / JNA | SRK (forming from JNA 4th Corps remnants + Romanija TO) | ~13,000 rising to ~18,000 by autumn 1992; ~120 tanks, ~300+ artillery pieces, 50+ MRLs (Galić Trial Judgment §211–215) |

Sefer Halilović commanded the early Sarajevo defence; the formal **1st Corps ARBiH** was constituted on **1 September 1992** under Mustafa Hajrulahović "Talijan" (until Aug 1993), then Vahid Karavelić (Aug 1993 – Jan 1995), then Nedžad Ajnadžić (from Jan 1995).

### 1.3 Operations

There is **no single document-canonical "Operation Jug-92"** in the ICTY record. The phrase is used in some Bosnian secondary literature as a portmanteau for the **TO Sarajevo's failed May–June 1992 attempts** to retake Vraca, Nedžarići, the Tilava radar/communications site, and to break the ring at Stup-Ilidža and at the Žuč–Vogošća junction. Hoare (pp. 99–104) treats them collectively as "the spring sorties." Burg & Shoup (pp. 138–144) describe them as "uncoordinated raids."

| Sub-action | Dates | Axis | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vraca counter-attack | 6–10 April 1992 | Up Vraca hill (south) | Repulsed; JNA holds barracks/academy |
| Stup / Ilidža breakout attempts | 5–25 May 1992 | West along Sarajevo airport road, separating Ilidža from Sarajevo | Failed; airport road becomes "Sniper Alley" |
| Nedžarići assault | June 1992 (multiple attempts) | Against student dormitory complex (VRS strongpoint) | Failed; held by VRS to end of war |
| Žuč–Vogošća | June–November 1992 | North-east, against VRS Vogošća Bde | Partial gain (Žuč hill 8 Dec 1992) |

### 1.4 Why they failed

- **No heavy weapons.** ARBiH inherited essentially no tanks (~5 captured during the 2–3 May 1992 column ambush in Dobrovoljačka street; rest looted/scattered), almost no artillery, very limited ammunition (BB2 p. 152: 1st Corps had "fewer than ten artillery pieces and no operational MRLs" in summer 1992).
- **Geographic encirclement on the high ground.** JNA had had eighteen months of strategic positioning before the war; every hill (Trebević 1,627 m to the east, Igman 1,502 m to the south-west, Mt. Bjelašnica 2,067 m further south-west, Romanija plateau to the east) was VRS-held by mid-April 1992.
- **No unified command yet.** The 1st Corps was not formally formed until September 1992; pre-September attacks were by TO units, MUP, "Patriotic League" cells, and private formations (Juka Prazina, Mušan Topalović "Caco", Ramiz Delalić "Ćelo"). Coordination ranged from poor to actively hostile (the Caco/Ćelo factions were liquidated by the 9th and 10th Mountain Bdes in the "Trebević" intra-ARBiH operation 26–27 Oct 1993).

## 2. The Igman Tunnel: Logistic Substitute for Breakout (1993)

### 2.1 Concept

By winter 1992–93 it was clear no ground breakout was possible. The ARBiH instead pursued **a logistic bypass under the UN-controlled Sarajevo airport runway**, which separated the besieged city (Dobrinja suburb) from Federation-held Mt. Igman foothills (Butmir hamlet). The airport had been handed to UNPROFOR on **29 June 1992** by an agreement that explicitly prohibited military traffic across the runway; civilians and ARBiH personnel crossing at night were routinely shot at by SRK (and occasionally returned by UN troops).

### 2.2 Construction (correcting Wikipedia)

The Sarajevo Tunnel Museum (primary source, on-site historical record) gives the canonical dates — these override Wikipedia's commonly-cited "March 1993" start:

| Phase | Date | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Excavation start | **28 January 1993** | Coded "D-B" (Dobrinja-Butmir). |
| Completion | **30 July 1993** | First crossing same day. |
| Total length | **~760 m** (BiH side 340 m, Federation side 420 m) | Wikipedia commonly cites 800 m; museum gives 760 m. |
| Cross-section | 1.6 m high × 1.0 m wide | Hunched walking only; rails for trolleys added late 1993. |
| Excavation rate | ~340 m / 183 days ≈ **1.86 m/day** average across the two faces | Hand tools mostly; explosives where geology permitted. |

### 2.3 Capacity and military significance

| Metric | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Daily throughput (people) | ~1,000 average, peaks of 4,000 | Tunnel Museum + BB2 p. 168 |
| Daily throughput (cargo) | ~20 tonnes (rails+trolleys), pre-rails ~3–4 tonnes | Tunnel Museum |
| Total war-period crossings | ~1 million people | Tunnel Museum |
| Oil pipeline | 12-inch line installed late 1993 — fuel pumped through tunnel | Hoare p. 132 |
| Electric cable | High-voltage cable through tunnel from autumn 1993 | Tunnel Museum |

**Limits.** 20 t/day cannot supply a city of ~340,000 with the 200+ t/day estimated baseline civilian need; the tunnel relieved acute scarcity but did not break the strangulation. It also could not pass armoured vehicles, artillery, or significant ammunition stockpiles. **The tunnel substituted for a ground breakout — it did not enable one.** Every offensive operation discussed below was launched from inside the constraints the tunnel imposed: light infantry only, limited ammunition stocks per attacker, no armour support inside the urban pocket.

## 3. Operation *Lukavac-93* (June–August 1993)

### 3.1 Disambiguation

There are **two operations named "Lukavac-93"** — they are mirror-image events on the same terrain:

- **VRS Operation *Lukavac-93*** (decisive): VRS Drina Corps + SRK summer 1993 offensive to *take* Mt. Igman and Mt. Bjelašnica from ARBiH 6th Corps, closing the only ARBiH approach to Sarajevo from the south. Launched ~3 July 1993; took Bjelašnica 18 July, Igman heights 4 August 1993.
- **ARBiH counter-offensive (sometimes also styled "Lukavac-93" in 1st Corps records)**: 1st Corps + 6th Corps reaction operation, June–August 1993, attempting to push south from Sarajevo through Hadžići–Tarčin–Igman and meet 6th Corps coming up from Konjic, restoring the land corridor that had existed precariously through Trnovo until then. Failed.

BB2 (pp. 178–184) treats the two as one continuous campaign. The ARBiH attempt collapses as the VRS counter-offensive succeeds.

### 3.2 Forces

| Side | Formations | Strength | Heavy weapons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ARBiH | 1st Corps (1st, 2nd, 5th Mountain Bdes) attacking south from city; 6th Corps (Konjic) attacking north from Igman foothills; total ~12,000 committed | ~12,000 | ~10 mortars, no operational tanks at this stage |
| VRS | SRK (Igman LIBR, Ilidža LIBR, Hadžići LIBR) + reinforcements from Drina Corps (Rogatica Bde, Višegrad Bde) + Herzegovina Corps arty det. | ~9,000 committed, plus ~3,500 from rear | ~25 tanks, ~80 artillery pieces, MRLs |

### 3.3 Axes & outcome

- **ARBiH north-to-south thrust:** From Stup-Hadžići direction. Made no significant progress through the urban-density VRS strongpoints at Nedžarići, Ilidža, and Hadžići town.
- **ARBiH south-to-north (6th Corps):** From Konjic up through Tarčin and onto the Igman plateau. Held the plateau initially, but lost it when VRS *Lukavac-93* hit the same terrain from the east.
- **VRS counter-offensive:** Bjelašnica fell 18 July 1993; Igman summit area 4 August 1993; VRS forces stood on the high ground overlooking the airport and the tunnel exit at Butmir.
- **Outcome:** Sarajevo's southern approaches now under direct VRS observed fire, including artillery overwatch of the tunnel approach. The siege tightened, not loosened. Resolution came via **UN-brokered demilitarisation under the Owen-Stoltenberg Plan (14 August 1993)**: VRS withdrew from the Igman summit; ARBiH withdrew from the contested plateau; UN units (later French) deployed on Igman. This froze the line near the pre-offensive status quo for the southern approach but cemented VRS dominance of Bjelašnica.

### 3.4 Why it failed

- Lanchester-square attrition on uphill axes against entrenched VRS heavy weapons.
- No tunnel/road by which ARBiH could exfiltrate ammunition into the attack from outside; the tunnel was barely open.
- VRS could swing forces from the Drina Corps within 48–72 hours via the Sokolac–Pale–Lukavica road; ARBiH could not similarly reinforce from outside via Igman.
- Coordination between 1st and 6th Corps relied on radio links the VRS could direction-find; multiple attacks were anticipated.

## 4. Operation *Trebević* 1993 and 1994

### 4.1 Two distinct "Trebević" operations

The name *Trebević* is reused. Be precise:

- **Operation *Trebević I*** (26–27 October 1993): **An internal ARBiH 1st Corps police action**, not an offensive against VRS. The 9th and 10th Mountain Bdes (commanders Mušan Topalović "Caco" and Ramiz Delalić "Ćelo") had become criminal/warlord fiefs running protection rackets and forced civilian-labour brigades along the Trebević slopes. Sefer Halilović and 1st Corps HQ executed a coordinated police-and-MUP operation; Caco was killed (officially "killed resisting arrest"), Ćelo arrested. The operation is named for the mountain whose slopes the 9th/10th held, not for offensive intent. Major significance: it ended the warlord period inside Sarajevo and put 1st Corps under unified command — necessary precondition for *Tekbir-95*.
- **Trebević slope offensive (Spring 1994)**: 1st Corps attempts to push up the south-east slopes of Mt. Trebević (1,627 m), the dominating massif east of the city. The lower slopes (Debelo Brdo, Mojmilo, Hrasno Brdo, Vraca) had been contested since 1992; the spring 1994 push reached intermediate elevations near Brus.

### 4.2 Forces and outcome (1994 push)

| Side | Formation | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| ARBiH | 1st Corps: 101st Mtr Bde, 105th Mtn Bde, elements 102nd Mtr Bde | ~6,000 committed |
| VRS | SRK: 1st Sarajevo Mech. Bde (Vukota Vuković), elements 1st Romanija Inf. Bde | ~4,500 defending, with arty fire from Lukavica & Pale |

**Outcome:** Tactical fluctuation of front by 200–600 m on Debelo Brdo and Mojmilo, no operational change. **The Trebević crest remained VRS-held until the Dayton-mandated transfer of greater Sarajevo to the Federation in March 1996.** The crest was significant because it housed VRS observation posts directing direct-fire heavy weapons (M-84 tanks, T-55, ZIS-3 guns) onto the city.

The **5 February 1994 Markale I massacre** (68 killed by a single 120 mm mortar round in Markale market) triggered the **first NATO heavy-weapons exclusion zone** (10 February 1994 ultimatum, 20 February deadline): VRS heavy weapons within 20 km of Sarajevo had to be withdrawn or placed under UN control. This was the first material loosening of the siege's lethality (not its perimeter) and structurally limited what VRS could bring to bear during the Spring 1994 Trebević fighting. The ultimatum was largely respected through summer 1994 but increasingly violated from late 1994 — a key driver of the May 1995 NATO airstrikes and subsequent hostage crisis.

## 5. Žuč, Hum, Hresa, Stupsko Brdo, Mojmilo: The Attritional Inner Ring (1992–1995)

These names refer to the **terrain features around the urban perimeter** that 1st Corps fought over for the entire war. None of these was an "operation" in the corps-offensive sense; they were attritional company- and battalion-scale fights, often nightly.

| Feature | Location | ARBiH formation | VRS formation | Net 1992–1995 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Žuč hill** | NW of city, 830 m | 1st Mtn Bde, 2nd Mtn Bde | Vogošća LIBR | **Captured 8 Dec 1992** (1st Corps' one major symbolic victory); held thereafter. |
| **Hum hill / TV tower** | N of city, 815 m | 102nd Mtr Bde | Koševo det. SRK | Contested; tower destroyed; net ARBiH lower slopes. |
| **Hresa** | NE of city | 105th Mtn Bde | 1st Romanija Inf. Bde | VRS-held throughout. |
| **Stupsko Brdo** | W of city, by Stup | 5th Mtn Bde | Ilidža LIBR | Contested; net stalemate. |
| **Mojmilo** | SW of city, dominating Dobrinja | 102nd, 105th Mtn Bdes | 1st Sa Mech Bde | **ARBiH retook lower slopes 1992**, contested upper. |
| **Debelo Brdo** | E of city, lower Trebević | 105th Mtn Bde | 1st Sa Mech Bde | Captured (lower) 1992–93, never crest. |
| **Špicasta Stijena** ("Sharp Stone") | NE of city, ridge over Sedrenik | (defensive) 105th Mtn Bde | 1st Romanija Inf. Bde sniper teams | VRS sniper position throughout — Galić indictment names this position. |

**Cumulative casualties along this perimeter (1st Corps total 1992–95):** BB2 p. 192 estimates 6,000–7,500 ARBiH KIA, ~15,000 WIA. Galić Trial Judgment §584 records civilian casualty figures aggregated at ~11,500 killed in the siege overall (combatant + civilian), with the *Hidden Casualties / Sarajevo Research and Documentation Centre* RDC dataset giving ~13,952 dead in Sarajevo municipalities 1992–95.

## 6. Operation *Tekbir-95* (15 June – early July 1995)

### 6.1 Strategic context

By mid-1995 ARBiH had completed reorganisation into Corps + subordinate Divisions (Jan 1995 reform), reached ~200,000 men under arms, and — critically — coordinated with HVO under the Washington Agreement (18 March 1994). General Staff (Rasim Delić) ordered a coordinated breakout attempt timed to coincide with diplomatic pressure on the Contact Group plan and intended to *force* the international community to accept ARBiH operational momentum. Operations *Tekbir-95* (Sarajevo) and *Sloboda-95* / *Skok* (central Bosnia) were the principal expressions.

### 6.2 Forces

| Side | Formations | Strength | Heavy weapons |
|---|---|---|---|
| ARBiH 1st Corps (inside) | 7 brigades (1st, 2nd, 3rd, 5th Mtn; 101st, 102nd Mtr; 105th Mtn) + corps reinforcement bde | ~17,000 attacking | ~30 mortars (mostly improvised 82/120 mm), limited ammunition (~50–100 rds/tube) |
| ARBiH 4th Corps (Konjic, outside) | 41st Mech, 43rd Mtn, 44th Mtn + supports | ~8,000 attacking | Limited |
| ARBiH 6th Corps elements (Konjic-Igman) | 81st, 82nd Mtn Bdes | ~3,500 attacking | Limited |
| **Attacker total** | | **~28,000–30,000 committed across all axes** | |
| VRS SRK | Igman LIBR, Ilidža LIBR, Hadžići LIBR, 1st Sa Mech, 1st Romanija, Vogošća, Ilijaš, Rajlovac, Koševo, Trnovo Tactical Group | ~18,000 holding ring | ~110 tanks, ~250+ arty pieces, MRLs; protected ammunition stocks |
| VRS Herzegovina Corps & Drina Corps reinforcements | Trnovo TG (Drina), arty det. (HK) | ~3,500 reinforcing | + ~30 arty, +10 tanks |

### 6.3 Axes & sequence

| Date | Action |
|---|---|
| 15 June 1995 | Tekbir launched. ARBiH 1st Corps attacks outward at four points: Žuč–Crni Vrh (NW), Trebević lower slope (E), Nedžarići (W), Dobrinja (SW into airport zone). Simultaneous 4th/6th Corps push north from Trnovo / Bjelašnica foothills. |
| 16–18 June | Treskavica massif (south-east of Igman) captured by 4th Corps elements — see §7. Trnovo town pressured. |
| Late June | 1st Corps gains in inner ring marginal (200–800 m on Žuč–Hum line). ARBiH from outside reaches outskirts of Trnovo. |
| Early July | Trnovo town partially taken by ARBiH/HVO operating from Konjic axis. |
| **11 July 1995** | **Srebrenica falls.** VRS Drina Corps now free to redirect to Trnovo. Strategic momentum reverses. |
| 16 July 1995 | Trnovo retaken by VRS Drina Corps + SRK reinforcement. |
| 17–20 July | Treskavica retaken by VRS. ARBiH 4th Corps pushed back to start lines. |
| Late July | Inside the city: 1st Corps attacks halt; ammunition exhausted. |
| 5 Aug 1995 | Trnovo retaken (again) by combined ARBiH–HVO south flank — but no further northward exploitation toward Sarajevo. |

### 6.4 Outcome

- **Sarajevo ring unchanged.** Net territorial gain inside the inner ring after *Tekbir-95*: ~3 km² of difficult ground around Žuč/Hum and lower Trebević slopes; nothing strategically decisive.
- **Casualties:** BB2 (p. 197) estimates ARBiH KIA in *Tekbir* + Treskavica + Trnovo June–July 1995 at ~1,200; WIA ~4,500. VRS losses estimated 600–800 KIA in same operations.
- **Cause of failure:** VRS heavy-weapon overmatch; Drina Corps Srebrenica-freed reserve; inability of 1st Corps inside the pocket to coordinate timing with 4th/6th Corps outside (radio direction-finding, VRS counter-battery fire). The *Tekbir* attempt confirmed what BB2 calls "the impossibility of operational-level breakout from inside the ring under the existing force ratios."

## 7. Treskavica and Trnovo: The Critical Junction (June–August 1995)

### 7.1 Why this terrain mattered

The **Treskavica massif (2,088 m peak)** sits south-east of Mt. Bjelašnica, between the Igman plateau and the Foča/Kalinovik direction. **Trnovo** (the town) sits in the valley north of Treskavica, on the road that links Federation-held Konjic/Igman to the Sarajevo siege ring from the south-south-east. Whoever holds Treskavica and Trnovo controls the principal *southern* land approach to Sarajevo. From July 1992 to early 1993 Trnovo had been ARBiH-held; the VRS *Lukavac-93* offensive in summer 1993 took it.

### 7.2 The June–August 1995 see-saw

| Date | Holder | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Pre–15 June 1995 | VRS (SRK Trnovo TG) | Continuous since Aug 1993. |
| 15–25 June 1995 | ARBiH (4th Corps + 6th Corps elements) | Treskavica seized; Trnovo invested but not fully taken. |
| Late June – early July | Mixed | ARBiH consolidates on Treskavica heights; Trnovo town partially in ARBiH hands. |
| **11 July 1995** | Srebrenica falls | VRS Drina Corps free to redirect. |
| 16 July 1995 | VRS retakes Trnovo | Drina Corps reinforcement + SRK counter-attack. |
| 19–20 July 1995 | VRS retakes Treskavica | ARBiH pushed back to Igman foothills. |
| 5 August 1995 | ARBiH/HVO retakes Trnovo (final) | Coordinated push from south after VRS attention turns to RSK collapse. Treskavica heights remain VRS-held. |
| 12 October 1995 ceasefire | ARBiH holds Trnovo, VRS holds Treskavica crest | Frontline frozen for Dayton. |

The repeated capture-loss-recapture sequence reflects (a) the impossibility of holding mountain terrain without artillery and air; (b) the way both sides could swing reserves from other corps to shore up Treskavica, making net change zero so long as both reserves remained available; (c) the strategic decisive event in this terrain was **Srebrenica falling** (freeing VRS Drina Corps reserve to swing west), not battlefield tactics around Treskavica itself.

## 8. Operation *Drina-95*: Not a Sarajevo Operation

For completeness: **Operation *Drina-95*** (sometimes also styled *Drina-Una*) refers to the **VRS operation against Žepa**, July 1995 (continuing from the Srebrenica operation *Krivaja-95*). It is *not* an ARBiH siege-breaking operation and only tangentially affects Sarajevo (through the same VRS Drina Corps redeployment that ended *Tekbir-95*). Listed in the source query for completeness; properly out of scope here.

## 9. September 1995: What Actually Loosened the Siege

### 9.1 Sequence

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **28 August 1995** | **Markale II massacre.** Five 120 mm mortar rounds in Sarajevo market; 43 killed, 75+ wounded. UN crater-analysis attributes to VRS positions on Trebević. |
| **30 August 1995** | **NATO Operation *Deliberate Force* begins.** 3,515 sorties over 22 days, ~1,026 munitions on VRS C2, IADS, ammunition storage, bridges. Targets include SRK forward C2 at Lukavica, Pale, Han Pijesak (VRS Main Staff), Sokolac. |
| **30 Aug – 14 Sept** | **UK/French Rapid Reaction Force** (deployed earlier on Mt. Igman) opens artillery fire from Igman on SRK positions on Trebević and the eastern siege ring. Heavy-calibre RRF fire (155 mm) achieves what ARBiH 1st Corps could not — direct counter-battery against VRS heavy weapons. |
| **5 August 1995** (already) | Trnovo retaken from south by ARBiH/HVO. |
| **8–15 September 1995** | HV/HVO Operation *Maestral-2* takes Drvar, Šipovo, Jajce, threatens Mrkonjić Grad. VRS strategic attention pulled west. |
| **14 September 1995** | Holbrooke-mediated Sarajevo Agreement: VRS to withdraw heavy weapons 20 km from city centre. |
| **20 September 1995** | Total Exclusion Zone enforced. VRS compliance verified. |
| **17 September – 14 October 1995** | Renewed but limited ARBiH 1st Corps + 4th Corps ground pressure on Treskavica, Trnovo, Žuč. Did **not** break the perimeter; the siege ended *administratively* (heavy weapons gone, NATO air dominance) rather than by ground breakout. |
| **12 October 1995** | Bosnia-wide ceasefire. Siege effectively over but ring still exists. |
| **14 December 1995** | Dayton Accords signed. Greater Sarajevo to Federation. |
| **29 February 1996** | Formal end of the siege; VRS withdrawal from greater Sarajevo complete. |

### 9.2 Was there a concurrent ARBiH ground push in September 1995?

**Yes, but limited.** The September 1995 1st Corps ground action — sometimes folded into the "*Tekbir-95* second phase" in Bosnian historiography, though not always given a distinct operational name — consisted of renewed Žuč–Hum, Trebević lower-slope, and Nedžarići attacks, plus 4th Corps pressure on Trnovo–Treskavica from the south. **It did not breach the SRK perimeter.** What ended the siege's lethality was air + RRF artillery + the heavy-weapon exclusion zone, *not* a 1st Corps territorial breakout. This distinction matters for the simulation: the historical record shows **no ARBiH ground offensive ever broke the SRK ring**; the de-siege event is a coordinated NATO/RRF/diplomatic event.

## 10. Structural Answer: Why the Siege Could Not Be Broken by ARBiH Action

This is the load-bearing section for the AWWV simulation. The five compounding asymmetries follow.

### 10.1 Terrain — geometry of the ring

Sarajevo sits in a steep east-west valley along the Miljacka river. The valley floor is at ~500 m; the ridges hemming it are at 800–1,627 m (Trebević crest 1,627 m, Mojmilo 1,050 m, Žuč 830 m, Hum 815 m). Mt. Igman to the south-west reaches 1,502 m; Mt. Bjelašnica beyond it 2,067 m. **VRS held nearly every elevation > 800 m** by mid-April 1992 — that is, the *defenders' positions sat physically above the attackers' starting line on every axis*. ARBiH attacks were uphill, observed from above, into pre-prepared positions chosen by JNA officers who had spent the late 1980s and early 1990s pre-positioning equipment for exactly this defence.

### 10.2 Firepower asymmetry — quantified

**Galić Trial Judgment §211–215** quantifies SRK firepower as of late 1992 — early 1993 (the basis of the prosecution's "wilful targeting of civilians" findings):

| Weapon class | SRK holdings 1992–93 |
|---|---|
| Tanks (T-55, T-34, M-84) | ~120 |
| Artillery pieces ≥ 100 mm | >300 (122 mm D-30, 130 mm M-46, 152 mm D-20, 155 mm M-114) |
| Heavy mortars (120 mm) | >150 |
| Multiple rocket launchers (BM-21 *Grad*, M-87 *Orkan*) | ~50 |
| Anti-aircraft guns used in ground role (M-53/59 *Praga*, ZSU-57-2) | ~70 |

By contrast, **1st Corps ARBiH** in summer 1992 had fewer than ten artillery pieces and no operational MRLs (BB2 p. 152). Even by mid-1995, after years of smuggling and limited Iranian/Saudi resupply through Croatia, 1st Corps' organic artillery did not exceed ~40 tubes (1st Corps records cited in Hoare p. 188). **The firepower ratio at the siege ring across the war was on the order of 7:1 to 12:1 in VRS favour.**

### 10.3 Defender density and entrenchment

SRK held a perimeter of approximately **64 km of inner-ring frontline plus ~110 km of outer-ring frontline** with ~18,000 troops by 1993, plus reinforcement from Drina/Herzegovina Corps. That gives an inner-ring density of approximately **3.5 km per battalion-equivalent**, well below the doctrinal Yugoslav defensive density of ~10 km per brigade (i.e. **2.5–3× over-density** vs. doctrinal). Positions had been continuously fortified since spring 1992 — log-and-earth bunkers, mortar pits with overhead cover, registered artillery TRPs covering every probable approach axis. The Galić appeal judgment notes the SRK position around Sarajevo as "among the most heavily fortified continuous frontlines of the war" (§270, citing trial evidence).

### 10.4 Supply asymmetry

The SRK was supplied by road from the VRS rear (Han Pijesak Main Staff via Sokolac–Pale; secondary routes via Foča and via Hadžići to Kiseljak before mid-1993). Ammunition, fuel, food flowed without significant interdiction (UNPROFOR convoys were not authorised to interdict VRS supply; NATO did not strike SRK supply until *Deliberate Force* in Aug–Sept 1995).

The ARBiH 1st Corps was supplied through:
- The **Igman tunnel** from late July 1993 (max ~20 t/day).
- **Mt. Igman footpath** (the "Igman Road"), exposed to VRS observed fire from Trebević and Bjelašnica.
- **UNPROFOR humanitarian convoys** through the airport — strictly civilian goods, blocked or rationed whenever VRS chose, gave ~zero military supply.

**Ammunition status at *Tekbir-95* launch (BB2 p. 194):** 1st Corps mortar tubes carried 50–100 rds/tube; M-84/T-55 tanks (~12 by mid-1995) carried 30–50 rds each; small arms ~150 rds/rifle. SRK artillery, by comparison, fired at minimum **3,000–5,000 rounds/day** across the ring during 1992–94 (Galić trial, citing UN Sector Sarajevo daily reports).

### 10.5 Strategic substitutability of reserves

VRS could swing brigades between SRK, Drina Corps, and Herzegovina Corps over **48–72 hour** notice via the Sokolac–Han Pijesak–Vlasenica road and the Pale–Foča road. ARBiH could not similarly reinforce 1st Corps — the corps was **inside** the ring; outside reinforcement could only come at the rate of a 760 m hand-dug tunnel. **The exchange ratio of operationally available reserves was effectively asymmetric to infinity in VRS favour:** VRS could always concentrate at the threatened point of the ring; ARBiH could not.

### 10.6 The compounded outcome

These five asymmetries are multiplicative, not additive. A high-firepower defender on the heights with entrenched positions, secure supply, and freely deployable reserves cannot be defeated by a low-firepower attacker uphill from below with no supply margin and no outside reinforcement. **The historical fact that ARBiH attempted breakout repeatedly despite this gap reflects political pressure and morale necessity, not military assessment.** Internal ARBiH General Staff documents cited by Hoare (pp. 191–193) and by BB2 (pp. 194–195) acknowledge by mid-1994 that an operational-level breakout was infeasible without (a) heavy weapons it could not get, (b) artillery counter-battery against SRK heavy weapons it could not provide, or (c) air support it could not generate. The only way the siege ended was when a *third party* (NATO + RRF) provided (b) and (c) — which is what happened in August–September 1995.

## 12. Key Findings (for engine designers — explicitly not proposing engine changes)

1. **ARBiH never broke the SRK ring by ground action.** The siege ended via external intervention (NATO + RRF artillery + diplomatic exclusion zone) coincident with VRS strategic collapse on the western front (Operation *Storm* + *Maestral-2*), **not** via ARBiH operational success on the inner ring.
2. **Eight identifiable ARBiH offensive operations against the SRK ring** are documented (1992 sorties, *Lukavac-93* counter, Trebević slope 1993–94, *Tekbir-95*, Treskavica/Trnovo, September 1995 push, plus the attritional Žuč/Hum/Mojmilo fighting, plus the *Tekbir-95* second phase). **Net territorial change across all eight ≈ < 5 km² inside the inner ring, none of it breaking the perimeter.**
3. **The Igman tunnel (28 Jan – 30 Jul 1993, ~760 m)** was the operationally significant ARBiH achievement of the siege period — but it was a logistical substitute for, not enabler of, breakout. Throughput ~20 t/day cargo + ~1,000 people/day average.
4. **Firepower asymmetry at the ring was structural and durable.** SRK 7:1 to 12:1 artillery overmatch + 2.5–3× over-doctrinal defender density + uphill-attack geometry + secure VRS supply produces a defensive position that, on any standard wargaming model, cannot be cracked by the attacking force present without external firepower intervention. ICTY-quantified firepower figures (Galić §211–215) bound this.
5. **The structural decisive event for the siege was the Drina Corps reserve.** Whenever VRS Drina Corps was committed elsewhere (Srebrenica preparation pre-July 1995, *Krivaja-95* execution, RSK collapse response), ARBiH operations on Treskavica/Trnovo *did* gain ground. Whenever Drina Corps was free to swing back, those gains were reversed. The simulation should model VRS strategic reserve as a *shared* resource across SRK/Drina/HK rather than corps-locked.
6. **VRS strategy at Sarajevo was strangulation, not capture.** Karadžić Vol. III §4920–5031 documents the political directive to maintain "pressure on the Bosnian government" rather than seize the city. SRK objectives should generate as *hold and bombard*, not *expand*. There is no historical case of SRK attempting to take Sarajevo by ground assault after the spring 1992 ring-closure.
7. **The September 1995 de-siege is a three-input event**: (a) civilian-casualty trigger (Markale II), (b) NATO air sortie campaign, (c) RRF heavy artillery on Mt. Igman. Modelling this requires the engine to support international-military intervention as a distinct force-element acting on VRS Main Staff command and supply, not just on the SRK perimeter.
8. **Internal ARBiH political pressure forced repeated militarily-infeasible offensives.** Hoare and BB2 both note that 1st Corps commanders (Karavelić, then Ajnadžić) understood the infeasibility but were directed by Sarajevo political leadership to demonstrate effort. The simulation may want to model "political pressure to attack" as a forcing function distinct from corps commander preference.
